## High-Volume Post-Application Rejections

> You are an HR Manager sending a rejection email to a candidate who applied but did not progress past the initial screening stage.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Department: {department}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Reason for rejection (internal only, do not state directly): {rejection_reason: missing_required_qualification / volume_of_stronger_applicants / role_filled_internally}
> Tone: {tone: warm_and_brief / professional_and_neutral}
>
> Write a 120 to 150 word rejection email. Open by thanking the candidate by name for their time. Do not state the specific rejection reason. Include one genuine sentence acknowledging the competitive nature of the role. Close by wishing them well in their search. Do not invite them to reapply unless the tone variable is set to warm_and_brief — in that case, add a single sentence leaving the door open.

**When to use it:** Monday morning when you have 40 screening rejections to clear before your 10am hiring manager call.

**Pro tip:** If your ATS auto-sends rejections and you're overriding it with a manual send, delete the subject line ChatGPT generates — it often repeats the job title in a way that clashes with your ATS thread subject. Write your own one-liner subject separately.

> You are an HR Manager sending a batch of post-application rejection emails for a role that received unusually high volume.
>
> Role: {job_title}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Number of applications received (approximate): {application_count}
> Closing date of application window: {closing_date}
> Tone: {formal / conversational}
> Include employer brand line: {yes / no}
> Employer brand line (if yes): {brand_line}
>
> Write a single 100 to 130 word rejection email suitable for sending to all unsuccessful applicants at this stage. Acknowledge the volume of applications without making it sound like an excuse. Do not use the phrase "we regret to inform you". If the employer brand line is included, weave it naturally into the closing sentence rather than bolting it on at the end.

**When to use it:** After a role closes and you need one clean email you can personalise with a mail merge first-name field rather than writing 80 individual messages.

**Pro tip:** "We regret to inform you" flags as formulaic to candidates who receive a lot of rejections. ChatGPT will still include it unless you explicitly ban it — which is why this prompt does.

> You are an HR Manager rejecting a candidate who applied for a role that has been put on hold or cancelled entirely.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role they applied for: {job_title}
> Reason the role is on hold: {reason: budget_freeze / organisational_restructure / role_scope_changed / position_filled_differently}
> Can you share the reason externally: {yes / no}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Tone: {empathetic / professional_and_brief}
>
> Write a 130 to 160 word rejection email. If the reason can be shared externally, state it plainly in one sentence without over-explaining. If not, indicate the role is no longer moving forward without suggesting fault lies with the candidate. Do not use vague language like "circumstances beyond our control". Close with a specific offer: either invite them to a talent pool or wish them well — choose based on tone.

**When to use it:** When a hiring freeze hits mid-process and you have candidates in active screening who deserve a real explanation, not a copy-paste brush-off.

**Pro tip:** If you can share the reason (budget freeze, reorg), do it. Candidates who know why they were rejected for structural reasons are far more likely to reapply when the role reopens — and this prompt lets you say it without it sounding like an excuse.

> You are an HR Manager sending a post-application rejection to a candidate who is clearly underqualified for the role based on the job description requirements.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Missing qualification or experience (internal, do not quote directly): {gap: years_of_experience / specific_credential / technical_skill}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Is there a more suitable open role at your company: {yes / no}
> Suitable role (if yes): {suitable_role_title}
> Tone: {direct_and_kind}
>
> Write a 110 to 140 word rejection email. Do not list the candidate's shortcomings. If there is a suitable open role, mention it in one sentence with a genuine reason why it might be a better fit. If there is no suitable role, close with a forward-looking statement that does not sound hollow. Never use "unfortunately at this time".

**When to use it:** When a candidate has applied for a senior role with two years of experience and you want to redirect rather than just reject.

**Pro tip:** If you're redirecting to another open role, link directly to the job posting in the email rather than your careers page. Candidates who get redirected to a generic page rarely take the next step.

> You are an HR Manager sending a rejection to a candidate who applied speculatively (no open role exists) and whose CV is not a match for any current or near-term need.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Speculative application area: {area_of_interest}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Likelihood of relevant opening in next 6 months: {high / low / unknown}
> Tone: {genuine_and_brief}
> Add to talent pool: {yes / no}
>
> Write a 100 to 120 word response. Acknowledge their initiative in reaching out speculatively. Do not promise anything you cannot deliver. If likelihood of an opening is high or you are adding them to a talent pool, say so clearly and state what happens next (e.g. "we will reach out if a role matching your background opens"). If likelihood is low, close warmly without stringing them along.

**When to use it:** When a strong speculative CV lands in your inbox for a function where you have no headcount, and you want to respond properly rather than leave it on read.

**Pro tip:** "We'll keep your CV on file" is meaningless to candidates because most HR teams don't actually do it. Only write this if your ATS has a live talent pool and you are actively tagging this person in it right now.

## Post-First-Interview Rejections

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who completed a first-round interview but will not be progressing to the next stage.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Interviewer name: {interviewer_name}
> Interview format: {phone_screen / video_call / in_person}
> Primary reason for not progressing (internal): {reason: stronger_candidates_available / competency_gap / culture_fit / salary_mismatch}
> Offer brief feedback: {yes / no}
> Tone: {warm / professional}
>
> Write a 150 to 200 word rejection email. Thank the candidate by name and reference the interview format so it feels personal. Do not state the internal reason. If feedback is yes, include one constructive, specific sentence — not a generic comment. If feedback is no, acknowledge their effort without being vague. Close with a genuine wish for their search, not a boilerplate line.

**When to use it:** After a first-round debrief when you have four candidates to reject before sending second-round invites the same day.

**Pro tip:** If the real reason is salary mismatch, you can say "the role's compensation range was not aligned with expectations" without it being rude — candidates respect honesty about money more than a vague "we went with other candidates".

> You are an HR Manager sending a post-first-interview rejection to a candidate who interviewed well but was rejected purely because a stronger applicant exists for this specific role.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Something specific the candidate did well (from interviewer notes): {positive_observation}
> Other open roles this candidate might suit: {relevant_role / none}
> Tone: {encouraging_and_genuine}
>
> Write a 170 to 220 word rejection email. Open with the specific positive observation — name it clearly so it does not read as generic praise. Deliver the rejection cleanly in one sentence. If there is a relevant open role, pivot to it with a clear reason. If not, invite them to apply for future roles and mean it — write one sentence that explains why you are saying it. Do not use "you were a strong candidate but…" as the opening clause.

**When to use it:** When a genuinely good candidate loses out to someone marginally stronger, and you want to keep them warm for the next opening.

**Pro tip:** Specific praise ("your breakdown of the go-to-market strategy in Q3 was exactly the level of thinking we look for") keeps candidates warm far more effectively than "you interviewed very well". Make the interviewer give you one real note before you write this email.

> You are an HR Manager sending a rejection to a candidate who was interviewed but revealed a significant skills gap during the technical or competency portion of the interview.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Skill gap identified: {skill_gap}
> Did the interviewer give verbal feedback already: {yes / no}
> Tone: {direct_and_respectful}
> Company name: {company_name}
>
> Write a 140 to 180 word rejection email. If the interviewer already gave verbal feedback, acknowledge it briefly rather than repeating it in full. If not, name the gap clearly in one sentence without being harsh — frame it as a mismatch between current experience and the role's requirements, not a personal failing. Do not use softening language that obscures the message. Close with a constructive forward-looking sentence.

**When to use it:** After a technical interview where the candidate fell short on a specific, named skill and deserves to know why — especially if they will be job-searching for a while and would benefit from the clarity.

**Pro tip:** "We decided to move forward with other candidates" when the real reason is a skill gap is a disservice. Candidates who know exactly what they are missing can do something about it. Be specific — ChatGPT will be vague unless you put the actual skill gap in the variable.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who completed a first-round interview but whose salary expectations are materially above the role's budget.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Candidate's stated salary expectation: {candidate_expectation}
> Role's maximum budget: {role_budget}
> Gap assessment: {small_gap_worth_noting / significant_gap_not_bridgeable}
> Tone: {transparent_and_respectful}
> Company name: {company_name}
>
> Write a 130 to 160 word rejection email. State plainly that the role's compensation range and the candidate's expectations are not aligned. Do not apologise for the company's budget. If the gap is small and worth noting, say so and leave room for a conversation. If the gap is not bridgeable, close cleanly without false hope. Do not use the phrase "at this time" — it implies the salary will change when it will not.

**When to use it:** After a first-round screen where the candidate named a number that is 30% above your ceiling and you want to close the loop without wasting anyone's time.

**Pro tip:** Candidates who receive a clear, honest salary-mismatch rejection often refer other candidates to you. Vague rejections after a money conversation damage your reputation with that person permanently.

> You are an HR Manager sending a post-first-interview rejection to a candidate who is being rejected due to a cultural or team-fit assessment from the interviewer.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Interviewer's concern (internal summary only): {fit_concern}
> Can you share any feedback externally: {yes_general_only / no}
> Tone: {warm_but_clear}
> Company name: {company_name}
>
> Write a 130 to 160 word rejection email. Do not reference culture fit, personality, or any language that could be perceived as discriminatory. If you can share general feedback, frame it around working style alignment or team structure — keep it factual and brief. If you cannot share feedback, write a respectful closing that does not raise suspicion or invite argument. Never use the phrase "we didn't feel you were the right fit" — rewrite it entirely.

**When to use it:** After an interviewer flags a fit concern and you need to decline without creating a legal or PR exposure.

**Pro tip:** "Culture fit" rejection language is the most litigated rejection phrasing in employment law. If in doubt, use a volume-of-strong-applicants framing instead and do not reference fit at all. This prompt is built to navigate that exactly.

## Final-Stage and Late-Process Rejections

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who reached the final round of interviews but was not selected for the role.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Number of interview rounds completed: {rounds_completed}
> Time candidate spent in process (weeks): {weeks_in_process}
> Specific strength observed during the process: {observed_strength}
> Hiring manager name (to reference or not): {hiring_manager_name}
> Tone: {warm_and_direct}
> Company name: {company_name}
>
> Write a 200 to 260 word rejection email. Acknowledge the full length of the process explicitly — do not gloss over the time they invested. Name the specific strength you observed. Deliver the rejection in one clear sentence without softening it into ambiguity. Thank the hiring manager by name if appropriate. Close with a genuine and specific reason to stay in touch — not a generic "we'll keep you in mind". Do not use "we went with a candidate who was a better fit".

**When to use it:** The hardest email of the week — when you have to reject someone who made it to the final two and spent six weeks in your process.

**Pro tip:** Final-round candidates talk. If your email is boilerplate, it gets screenshotted and posted. If it names their actual strength and acknowledges their time, it often gets shared positively. The investment of 90 seconds in this prompt pays a meaningful employer brand dividend.
