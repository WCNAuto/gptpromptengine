## Post-Interview Rejection Emails

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email after a first-round interview.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_first_name}
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Interview stage completed: {interview_stage: e.g. phone screen / first panel / technical test}
> Primary reason for rejection (internal only, use to inform tone without stating explicitly): {rejection_reason: e.g. weak technical skills / culture fit concerns / stronger candidates available}
> Tone: {warm_and_brief / professional_and_formal / encouraging}
> Company name: {company_name}
>
> Write a 120 to 160 word rejection email. Open by thanking the candidate for their time at the {interview_stage}. Do not state the rejection reason directly. Use a single sentence to leave the door open for future roles only if the tone is warm_and_brief or encouraging. Close with a professional sign-off from the HR team. No bullet points. No vague praise like "impressed by your background" unless it's genuinely warranted.

**When to use it:** Monday morning after a Friday interview round, when you have six candidates to notify before 10 a.m. and a hiring manager pushing you for updates.

**Pro tip:** If {rejection_reason} is culture fit, keep the email shorter, not longer. Extra words signal discomfort and candidates read that. Stick to 120 words and keep the close neutral rather than encouraging a reapplication.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email after a final-round interview for a senior role.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_first_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Final round format: {panel_interview / presentation / case_study / executive_interview}
> Time invested by candidate: {approximate_time: e.g. 3 hours across two sessions}
> Specific thing they did well (genuine, one line): {genuine_positive}
> Reason for rejection (internal, shapes tone only): {rejection_reason}
> Hiring manager name (optional, for sign-off): {hiring_manager_name}
>
> Write a 200 to 250 word rejection email. Acknowledge the time the candidate invested. Name the one genuine positive — this must not sound generic. Explain without over-explaining that another candidate was selected. Do not compare candidates. Do not invite feedback requests. Close warmly. No bullet points.

**When to use it:** After rejecting a finalist who made it to the last two candidates. These emails require more care because the candidate often tells people in their network how you handled it.

**Pro tip:** Filling in {genuine_positive} with something real — not "your experience was impressive" — is the single biggest factor in whether this email lands well. Pull it from your interview notes before generating the draft.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who interviewed twice but was not selected because the role was restructured or cancelled.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_first_name}
> Role they interviewed for: {job_title}
> Number of interview rounds completed: {rounds_completed}
> Reason role was cancelled or restructured: {reason: e.g. headcount freeze / role merged with another / business pivot}
> Whether to invite them to future roles: {yes / no}
> Tone: {apologetic_and_direct / professional_and_neutral}
>
> Write a 180 to 220 word email. Be direct in the first two sentences: the role will not be filled. Do not bury this information. Acknowledge that they invested real time. If {yes}, include a specific invitation to future roles with an instruction on how to stay in touch. If {no}, close cleanly without a vague "we'll keep you on file" line. No bullet points.

**When to use it:** When a hiring freeze or reorg hits mid-process and you have candidates who completed multiple rounds. This is the email most HR teams delay sending — don't.

**Pro tip:** If {reason} is a headcount freeze, do not use that exact phrase in the email — it reads as deflection. Reframe it as the company pausing hiring in this area. ChatGPT will soften it further once you provide the reason in plain terms.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who was referred internally by a current employee.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_first_name}
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Internal referrer name: {referrer_name}
> Relationship (so tone matches): {referrer_relationship: e.g. direct colleague of candidate / manager / friend}
> Stage reached before rejection: {stage_reached}
> Tone: {warm / professional}
> Whether to mention referrer explicitly: {yes / no}
>
> Write a 150 to 190 word rejection email addressed to {candidate_first_name}. If {yes}, acknowledge the referral in one sentence without making it the focus of the email. Do not imply that the referral raised expectations that weren't met. Reject clearly in the first paragraph. Keep the second paragraph brief and forward-looking only if the stage reached was interview or beyond. No bullet points.

**When to use it:** Any time a referral doesn't make it through and you need to communicate the outcome before the referring employee hears about it through other channels.

**Pro tip:** Send this email before the referrer finds out informally. If {referrer_name} hears from the candidate before you've sent this, the internal relationship becomes awkward and it reflects poorly on the process.

>
> Candidate name: {candidate_first_name}
> Role applied for: {job_title}

>

If {high_volume_role}, acknowledge the competitive applicant pool in one short sentence. Do not invite them to reapply unless the screen-out reason is location or salary, in which case omit the reapply line entirely. Close with a professional sign-off. No bullet points. No filler phrases.

**When to use it:** At the end of a high-volume application window when you need to clear 40 to 200 pre-screen rejections in one session.

**Pro tip:** For {high_volume_role} rejections, generate one email, verify the tone, then use the same prompt five more times with different {candidate_first_name} values rather than personalising every email — the ROI is not there at volume.

## Rejection Emails for Sensitive Situations

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who has applied for the same role more than once.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_first_name}
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Number of times they have applied: {application_count}
> Previous stage reached on prior application(s): {prior_stage: e.g. not interviewed / phone screened / first round}
> Current stage reached: {current_stage}
> Whether to address the multiple applications directly: {yes / no}
> Tone: {direct / professional}
>
> Write a 140 to 180 word rejection email. If {yes}, acknowledge the repeated application in one neutral sentence — do not frame it negatively. Reject clearly. Do not suggest they apply again unless you genuinely want them to. If the answer to reapplying is no, omit that line entirely rather than using a vague close. No bullet points.

**When to use it:** When a candidate applies for the third time and you need to close the loop clearly without triggering a complaint or an awkward follow-up call.

**Pro tip:** If {application_count} is three or more and {current_stage} is pre-interview, add a note to your ATS after sending so future applications are flagged before they re-enter the pipeline. The email is only half the fix.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who became aggressive or rude during the interview process.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_first_name}
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Stage at which behaviour occurred: {stage: e.g. first panel / technical interview / reference check call}
> Nature of behaviour (internal only, shapes tone): {behaviour_description: e.g. raised voice at interviewer / sent hostile follow-up emails / was dismissive to junior panellist}
> Legal review required before sending: {yes / no}
> Tone: {neutral_and_brief}
>
> Write a 90 to 120 word rejection email. Do not mention the behaviour in the email. Do not explain the decision. Do not apologise. State clearly that the company will not be proceeding. Close with a neutral sign-off. The email must be factual, short, and legally defensible. No bullet points. No encouragement to reapply.

**When to use it:** After a candidate behaves badly and you need a written record of the rejection that cannot be used against the company in a dispute.

**Pro tip:** If {legal_review_required} is yes, do not send the ChatGPT draft without a legal or HR leadership review first. Use the output as a starting point, not a final document. This is one of the few cases where the draft is a first step, not a finish line.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to an internal employee who applied for an internal promotion or transfer and was not selected.
>
> Employee name: {employee_first_name}
> Role they applied for: {job_title}
> Their current role: {current_role}
> Whether they were interviewed: {yes / no}
> Key reason for non-selection (internal only): {reason: e.g. selected candidate had more direct experience / development gap identified / headcount preference changed}
> Whether a follow-up conversation has been or will be scheduled: {yes / no / already_happened}
> Tone: {supportive_and_direct / professional}
>
> Write a 170 to 210 word email. Acknowledge their application directly in the first sentence. Reject clearly in the first paragraph — do not soften it to the point of ambiguity. If {follow_up_conversation} is yes or already_happened, reference it in one sentence. Do not give feedback in the email itself — save that for the conversation. Close with a sentence that affirms their value in their current role. No bullet points.

**When to use it:** Before the internal candidate hears the news through another manager or a Slack message. Internal rejections handled badly are one of the top reasons high performers disengage.

**Pro tip:** Do not include feedback in this email even if you have it. Feedback delivered in writing without a conversation to follow it invites interpretation, and that interpretation is often wrong. Keep this email clean and let the conversation do the work.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who disclosed a disability, health condition, or personal circumstance during the process.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_first_name}
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Stage reached: {stage_reached}
> Disclosure made (internal context only, do not reference in email): {disclosure_detail}
> Reason for rejection unrelated to disclosure (internal only): {actual_rejection_reason}
> Tone: {warm_and_professional}
>
> Write a 150 to 190 word rejection email. Do not reference the disclosure in any form. Reject clearly. The rejection reason must not be implied or connected to any personal characteristic. Use language that is warm but unambiguous. Do not over-explain the decision. Close respectfully. No bullet points. The email must be legally neutral.

**When to use it:** Any time a candidate has disclosed something personal and you need to ensure the rejection email cannot be read as connected to that disclosure.

**Pro tip:** Before you paste the output, read it once asking: "Could this sentence be read as linked to the disclosure?" If yes, remove that sentence. The goal is an email that is kind but contains nothing that could anchor a discrimination claim.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who asked for feedback during the process and expects it in the rejection.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_first_name}
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Stage they reached: {stage_reached}
> Feedback you are willing to share in writing (1 to 2 points only): {written_feedback_points}
> Feedback you are not willing to share in writing: {off_limits_feedback}
> Tone: {constructive_and_professional}
> Whether to offer a call for more detailed feedback: {yes / no}
>
> Write a 190 to 240 word rejection email. Reject clearly in the first paragraph. In the second paragraph, include only the {written_feedback_points} — keep each point specific and actionable, not vague. Do not reference {off_limits_feedback} in any form. If {yes}, offer a brief call for additional discussion in one sentence with a clear instruction on how to book it. Close professionally. No bullet points in the body.

**When to use it:** When a candidate explicitly asked for feedback during their interview and you agreed — and now need to deliver on that without opening a door you don't want to open.

**Pro tip:** Keep {written_feedback_points} to things you would say verbally without hesitation. If you wouldn't say it out loud in a call, don't put it in writing. The written version always carries more weight than the verbal one.

## High-Volume and Time-Pressured Rejection Emails

> You are an HR Manager writing a short batch rejection email for candidates who applied online and did not meet the minimum requirements for the role.
>
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Minimum requirement that was not met: {unmet_requirement: e.g. right to work in the country / required certification / minimum years of experience}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Hiring period: {hiring_period: e.g. Q3 2026 / August 2026}
> Tone: {brief_and_respectful}
>
> Write a single 75 to 100 word rejection email suitable for sending to multiple candidates with the same disqualifier. Use "Dear {candidate_first_name}" as the salutation placeholder so I can do a mail merge. Thank them for applying. State that they do not meet the requirements for this role at this time. Do not state the specific requirement. Encourage them to check future openings on the company careers page. Close professionally. No bullet points. No personalisation beyond the name placeholder.

**When to use it:** After closing an application window for a high-volume role and needing to clear 50 or more disqualified applicants in a mail merge before end of day.

**Pro tip:** The {unmet_requirement} variable is for your context only — do not include it in the email. Stating the disqualifier in writing can create unnecessary back-and-forth from candidates who claim they do meet it. Keep the email criteria-neutral.
