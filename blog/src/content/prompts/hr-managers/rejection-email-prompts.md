## Post-Interview Rejections (Phone Screen and First Round)

> You are an HR Manager sending a rejection email after a phone screen.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role they applied for: {job_title}
> Stage they reached: phone screen
> Main reason for not progressing (internal use only, do not state directly): {internal_reason}
> One thing they did well in the screen: {positive_observation}
> Tone: {warm / neutral / formal}
> Company name: {company_name}
>
> Write a 120 to 150 word rejection email. Thank them for their time. Reference the positive observation in a specific, genuine way — not a generic compliment. Decline without giving the internal reason or a detailed explanation. Leave the door open for future roles if appropriate. Do not use the phrase "we have decided to move forward with other candidates" — rewrite that idea in plain language. No bullet points. Signed off from the HR team, not an individual.

**When to use it:** Monday morning after Friday phone screens, when you have six rejections to send before 10am.

**Pro tip:** If the internal reason is "salary expectations too high," never hint at it — even vaguely. Mentioning budget at all invites negotiation replies that waste everyone's time.

> You are an HR Manager sending a rejection email after a first-round video interview.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Interview format: {video / in-person}
> Specific strength they showed: {strength_observed}
> Gap that ruled them out (do not state directly): {gap}
> Time since interview: {days_since_interview} days
> Tone: {empathetic / professional / brief}
> Company name: {company_name}
>
> Write a 150 to 180 word rejection email. Acknowledge the time since the interview if it has been more than 5 days, and apologise briefly for the delay — one sentence only. Name the strength they showed in a way that feels earned. Decline clearly without citing the gap. Close with a forward-looking sentence that does not feel hollow. No bullet points. Plain prose.

**When to use it:** When you're running two weeks behind on post-interview communications and need to acknowledge the delay without over-explaining it.

**Pro tip:** If {days_since_interview} is over 14, the apology sentence carries more weight than the rest of the email. Let ChatGPT write it, then read it aloud — if it sounds rehearsed, prompt it to try again with "write this more like a real person and less like a policy statement."

> You are an HR Manager rejecting a candidate after a first-round panel interview.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Panel feedback summary (internal, do not quote directly): {panel_feedback_notes}
> One area of genuine strength: {genuine_strength}
> Hiring decision: rejected at this stage, not blacklisted
> Tone: {warm / formal}
> Future pipeline interest: {yes / no}
> Company name: {company_name}
>
> Write a 160 to 200 word rejection email. Open by thanking them for attending the panel — name it as a panel, not a generic "interview." Reference their strength in one concrete sentence. Decline without paraphrasing the panel feedback. If future pipeline interest is yes, include one specific sentence inviting them to stay connected — not a generic "we'll keep your CV on file." If no, close warmly but do not make promises. No bullet points.

**When to use it:** After a panel interview where multiple interviewers had mixed views — the email needs to feel unified, not hesitant.

**Pro tip:** Avoid letting ChatGPT reference the panel size or names of interviewers unless you've briefed all panellists. Candidates sometimes follow up with individual panellists directly if they know who was in the room.

> You are an HR Manager sending a rejection email to a candidate who asked for feedback after their first-round interview.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Their request: they emailed asking for feedback
> Feedback you are willing to share (one or two points only): {shareable_feedback}
> Feedback you cannot share (legal or policy reason): {withheld_reason}
> Tone: {honest / diplomatic}
> Company name: {company_name}
>
> Write a 180 to 220 word email. Acknowledge their request directly in the first sentence. Share the {shareable_feedback} as specific, actionable observations — not vague praise or vague criticism. Do not reference the withheld reason or hint at it. If the feedback could apply to any candidate, rewrite it to feel specific to this person. Close by wishing them well in their search without using the phrase "best of luck." No bullet points.

**When to use it:** When a candidate has chased feedback and you want to respond genuinely without exposing the company to a follow-up dispute.

**Pro tip:** Run the output through your legal lens before sending. If {shareable_feedback} touches on communication style, technical gaps, or cultural fit, have your tone reflect developmental observation rather than character judgement. The word "fit" in a rejection email can invite discrimination queries in some jurisdictions.

> You are an HR Manager rejecting a strong first-round candidate who is not right for this role but who you want to consider for a different open position.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role they applied for: {applied_role}
> Role you want to redirect them to: {alternative_role}
> Why the redirect makes sense: {redirect_rationale}
> Tone: {enthusiastic / measured}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Hiring manager for the alternative role: {alt_hiring_manager_name}
>
> Write a 170 to 200 word email. Open by declining the {applied_role} clearly — do not bury it or delay it past the first two sentences. Pivot to the {alternative_role} with genuine reasoning based on {redirect_rationale}. Name {alt_hiring_manager_name} and give the candidate a clear next step. Do not make the redirect feel like a consolation prize. No bullet points.

**When to use it:** When a strong candidate applied for the wrong level or function and you have a better-matched open role you'd lose them to a competitor otherwise.

**Pro tip:** Only use this prompt when the alternative role is real and actively hiring. Redirecting a candidate to a role that goes cold in two weeks is worse for your employer brand than a clean rejection.

## Final-Round and Offer-Stage Rejections

> You are an HR Manager sending a rejection email to a candidate who reached the final round but was not selected.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Rounds they completed: {rounds_completed}
> Time invested by candidate (approximate): {time_invested}
> Reason for rejection (internal only, do not state): {internal_reason}
> One specific strength shown across the process: {process_strength}
> Tone: {warm / direct}
> Company name: {company_name}
>
> Write a 200 to 250 word rejection email. Open by acknowledging the number of rounds and the time they invested — name {rounds_completed} specifically. Express genuine regret without overstating it. Reference {process_strength} in one well-crafted sentence that names the behaviour, not just the trait. Decline clearly. Do not offer false comfort ("it was incredibly close"). Close with a specific and non-hollow offer — either to keep them in mind for a named type of future role, or to connect on LinkedIn. No bullet points.

**When to use it:** After a final-round decision, same day the internal decision is confirmed — not three days later.

**Pro tip:** Final-round rejections have the highest risk of generating a negative Glassdoor review. The sentence that lands hardest is usually the opener — if it leads with "unfortunately," the rest of the email reads as procedural. Ask ChatGPT to rewrite the opener without using "unfortunately" or "regret to inform."

> You are an HR Manager sending a rejection email to a candidate after an offer was extended but the hiring decision was reversed due to a role being put on hold.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Offer stage: offer {verbally offered / formally issued}
> Reason for hold (share only what is appropriate): {shareable_reason}
> Likelihood of role reopening: {likely / uncertain / unlikely}
> Tone: {apologetic / transparent / formal}
> Company name: {company_name}
>
> Write a 220 to 260 word email. Open by addressing the situation directly — do not soften it to the point of confusion. Name what happened (offer extended, role now on hold) in plain English. Share {shareable_reason} without oversharing internal business context. If reopening is likely, give a specific timeframe or commit to follow-up within a named number of weeks. If uncertain or unlikely, say so honestly. Close with a concrete action you will take (e.g. personal follow-up call, referral to another team). Do not use "due to circumstances beyond our control." No bullet points.

**When to use it:** The day the business decision is confirmed. Waiting even 24 hours after a formal offer has been issued creates legal and reputational exposure.

**Pro tip:** If a formal offer letter was issued, loop in your legal or employment counsel before sending this email. The prompt produces the right tone, but the specific wording around "offer rescinded" vs "role paused" can have different legal implications depending on your jurisdiction.

> You are an HR Manager rejecting a final-round internal candidate who applied for a promotion and was not selected.
>
> Employee name: {employee_name}
> Current role: {current_role}
> Role they applied for: {promotion_role}
> Decision: not selected, remaining in current role
> Reason (do not state directly): {internal_reason}
> Manager involvement in delivering message: {manager_involved: yes / no}
> Tone: {supportive / direct}
> Company name: {company_name}
>
> Write a 200 to 240 word email to be sent from HR (not from their manager). Acknowledge that this is a difficult message to receive. Recognise what they brought to the process without patronising them. If {manager_involved} is yes, name that their manager will follow up with a development conversation this week — give a specific timeframe. If no, include a brief sentence inviting them to set up time with HR directly. Do not make promises about future promotion cycles. No bullet points.

**When to use it:** Immediately after the internal hiring decision is finalised, and before the successful candidate's appointment is announced to the team.

**Pro tip:** The timing on this one matters more than the wording. If the internal candidate hears the news through the grapevine before this email lands, no amount of good writing recovers the trust. Send within two hours of the decision being made.

> You are an HR Manager sending a rejection email to a candidate who had a strong final round but lost out to one other candidate by a narrow margin.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Margin of decision: genuinely close
> What distinguished the selected candidate (do not mention directly): {distinguishing_factor}
> Likelihood of future openings in this area: {high / low / uncertain}
> Tone: {genuine / warm / professional}
> Company name: {company_name}
>
> Write a 180 to 220 word email. Do not say "it was an incredibly difficult decision" — convey closeness of the decision through specificity, not cliché. Reference one real quality the candidate demonstrated. Decline clearly and without hedging. If future openings are high likelihood, make a specific, credible offer to stay in touch — name a role type or business area. If low or uncertain, do not make promises. Close in one sentence. No bullet points.

**When to use it:** When the hiring manager genuinely rated two candidates almost equally and the rejected candidate deserves a response that reflects that — not a standard rejection.

**Pro tip:** Do not let ChatGPT write "we went with someone who had slightly more experience in X" even if that is true. That sentence invites a reply asking you to define "more experience" and starts a conversation you don't want.

> You are an HR Manager sending a post-offer rejection to a candidate who declined your offer, then reapplied six months later for the same role.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> History: declined your offer {months_ago} months ago, now reapplied
> Current hiring decision: {progressing / rejecting}
> If rejecting, the reason (internal, do not state directly): {internal_reason}
> Tone: {matter-of-fact / warm}
> Company name: {company_name}
>
> Write a 150 to 180 word email. Acknowledge that they've applied again without making it awkward. If progressing, invite them to the next stage clearly. If rejecting, decline without referencing their earlier decision to decline your offer — that is not the reason and should not be implied. Keep the tone professional regardless of the internal view on the re-application. No bullet points.

**When to use it:** When a boomerang candidate situation arises and you want to make sure the email reads as neutral and process-driven, not as "we remember what you did."

**Pro tip:** Before running this prompt, confirm with the hiring manager whether the previous decline is a factor in the current decision. If it is, that's a separate legal and HR conversation — this prompt should only go out when the rejection is genuinely based on current-cycle assessment.

## High-Volume and Early-Stage Rejections

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to send to candidates who applied but were screened out at the CV review stage without an interview.
>
> Role: {job_title}
> Number of applicants in this batch: {applicant_count}
> Application window: {application_open_date} to {application_close_date}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Tone: {professional / brief / warm}
> Any specific note to include (e.g. encouragement to apply for future roles): {optional_note}
>
> Write a 100 to 130 word rejection email suitable for sending to all applicants who were not shortlisted at CV stage. Do not make it feel automated, but do not over-personalise it — this will be sent at volume. Thank them for their application. Acknowledge the competition level briefly if {applicant_count} is high. Decline clearly without explanation. Include {optional_note} if provided. Do not use "we will keep your details on file" unless your company policy requires it. No bullet points.

**When to use it:** After closing an application window, when you need to reject 80-plus candidates before the shortlist interviews begin.

**Pro tip:** For GDPR compliance, do not include "we will keep your CV on file" unless your retention policy and privacy notice explicitly allow it. If your ATS auto-inserts that line, override it manually before sending.
