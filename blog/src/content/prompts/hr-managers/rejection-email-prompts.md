## Post-Interview Rejection Emails

> You are an HR manager writing a candidate rejection email after a final-round interview.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role they applied for: {job_title}
> Interview stage they reached: {interview_stage} (e.g. final panel, second-round technical, hiring manager call)
> Primary reason for not progressing: {rejection_reason} (e.g. stronger candidate selected, culture alignment, technical skill gap)
> Tone: {tone} (warm / neutral / formal)
> Did they interview well overall: {positive_interview_note} (yes — include a genuine compliment / no — keep neutral)
>
> Write a 150 to 200 word rejection email. Open by thanking them for their time at the {interview_stage}. State clearly that we are not moving forward. If {positive_interview_note} is yes, name one specific strength they demonstrated. Do not use vague phrases like "we had many strong candidates" more than once. Close with a genuine, non-hollow wish for their search. Do not invite them to reapply unless explicitly instructed.

**When to use it:** Friday afternoon when you have five post-final-round rejections to send before the weekend and each candidate invested real time preparing.

**Pro tip:** If the candidate made it to the hiring manager's panel, paste the hiring manager's verbal feedback into {rejection_reason}. ChatGPT will reflect that specificity back in a way that feels personal rather than automated — candidates notice the difference and it protects your employer brand.

> You are an HR manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who completed a take-home skills assessment but did not advance to interview.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Assessment type: {assessment_type} (e.g. written case study, coding task, data analysis exercise)
> Time investment from candidate: {time_investment} (e.g. approximately 3 hours)
> Reason for rejection: {rejection_reason} (e.g. output did not meet the technical threshold, presentation clarity below benchmark)
> Tone: {tone} (warm / neutral)
>
> Write a 120 to 160 word rejection email. Acknowledge the time they spent on the {assessment_type} directly in the first sentence — be specific about the effort, not generic. Decline clearly in sentence two. Give one brief, honest sentence on where the output fell short using {rejection_reason}, phrased constructively. Do not promise feedback calls unless you are prepared to follow through. Close warmly.

**When to use it:** When a candidate invested hours in a task assessment and a generic "we've decided not to proceed" response would feel dismissive or damage referral reputation.

**Pro tip:** Avoid phrasing like "your submission showed promise but..." if you have no intention of offering a feedback call — it creates inbound reply pressure. Instead, use "the submission didn't meet the benchmark we needed for this role at this time" — it's honest and closed.

> You are an HR manager writing a post-first-interview rejection email to a candidate who was screened by HR but not progressed to the hiring manager.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Interview format: {interview_format} (e.g. 30-minute video screening call, phone screen)
> Stage reached: HR screen / first-round only
> Reason not progressing: {rejection_reason} (e.g. salary expectations misaligned, role scope mismatch, requisite experience not present)
> Time between interview and this email: {days_since_interview} days
> Tone: {tone} (neutral / warm)
>
> Write a 100 to 140 word rejection email. If {days_since_interview} is more than 10, acknowledge the delay in one brief sentence. Decline clearly without over-explaining. Do not mention internal hiring dynamics or headcount. If {rejection_reason} involves salary, acknowledge alignment wasn't possible without disclosing the band. Close with two sentences maximum — no hollow calls to stay in touch unless it's genuine.

**When to use it:** Monday morning clear-out of first-round screens from last week that have been sitting without a decision email.

**Pro tip:** If the delay is over 14 days, do not open with "I hope you're well" — it reads as oblivious. A single direct line like "I'm sorry for the delay in getting back to you" lands better and disarms frustration before the rejection lands.

> You are an HR manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who progressed to a panel interview with three senior stakeholders but was ultimately not selected.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Interviewers on panel: {panel_composition} (e.g. VP of Engineering, Head of Product, HR Business Partner)
> Decision rationale: {decision_rationale} (e.g. another candidate's domain expertise was a closer match to the immediate roadmap)
> One genuine strength observed: {candidate_strength} (e.g. structured problem-solving, clear communication under pressure)
> Tone: {tone} (warm / formal)
> Would you welcome future applications: {future_applications} (yes / no)
>
> Write a 200 to 250 word rejection email. Open by naming the panel and thanking them for the depth of engagement across the process. Communicate the decision in plain language — do not bury it after two paragraphs. Name {candidate_strength} specifically in a way that feels observed, not generic. State {decision_rationale} in one sentence without revealing internal deliberations. If {future_applications} is yes, extend the invitation with a specific role type, not a vague "keep in touch".

**When to use it:** When a candidate has gone through a rigorous multi-stage process and a one-liner rejection would be a brand risk — especially for senior roles where candidates talk to each other.

**Pro tip:** For director-level and above rejections, send from the hiring manager's name, not HR. Using an HR sender for a C-1 rejection signals the process didn't value them. Adjust the prompt's sign-off accordingly.

> You are an HR manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who applied internally for a promotion and was not selected.
>
> Employee name: {employee_name}
> Current role: {current_role}
> Role they applied for: {target_role}
> Hiring manager's name: {hiring_manager_name}
> Reason for not selecting: {rejection_reason} (e.g. external candidate had direct leadership experience in the function, internal candidate needs 6 to 12 more months of scope)
> Next step offered: {next_step} (e.g. development conversation with {hiring_manager_name}, no formal next step at this time)
> Tone: warm and direct
>
> Write a 180 to 220 word rejection email. This person is still an employee — do not write this like an external rejection. Open by acknowledging the courage it takes to apply internally. Communicate the decision clearly in the second paragraph. State {rejection_reason} honestly but constructively — this person will hear the full story from their manager anyway, so vague language does more damage. If {next_step} exists, close with it as a concrete commitment, not a soft offer.

**When to use it:** After an internal promotion process closes and the employee's line manager has already had the verbal conversation — this email confirms and documents what was said.

**Pro tip:** If the verbal conversation hasn't happened yet, do not send this email first. The employee should hear a rejection from their manager before reading it in their inbox. Reorder the sequence, then deploy this prompt.

## High-Volume and Early-Stage Rejection Emails

> You are an HR manager writing a rejection email to candidates who applied for a role but did not meet the minimum qualifications and were screened out before any interview contact.
>
> Role: {job_title}
> Department: {department}
> Disqualifying criteria: {disqualifying_criteria} (e.g. right-to-work requirement not met, minimum years of experience not met, required qualification absent)
> Number of candidates receiving this email: {batch_size}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Tone: respectful / neutral
>
> Write a 80 to 110 word rejection email suitable for sending to {batch_size} candidates. Personalise with the candidate's first name at the open using a merge-field placeholder {{first_name}}. Do not mention the disqualifying criteria directly — keep it role-fit language. Thank them for their interest in {company_name} and the {job_title} role. Decline clearly. Wish them well. Do not include "we'll keep your CV on file" unless this is true and actioned.

**When to use it:** After closing applications on a high-volume role where 60% of applicants did not meet basic screening criteria and each one still deserves a response within the SLA.

**Pro tip:** Batch rejections at this stage are where "we received an overwhelming response" starts to sound hollow — candidates know it means unreviewed. Replace it with "your application was reviewed against the core requirements for this role." It's still brief, but it's honest.

> You are an HR manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who submitted a speculative CV with no open role available.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> CV specialty or area they highlighted: {candidate_specialty} (e.g. enterprise SaaS sales, supply chain analytics, UX research)
> Current hiring status in that area: {hiring_status} (e.g. no open roles currently, potential pipeline for Q4, actively hiring in adjacent area)
> Company name: {company_name}
> Tone: {tone} (warm / neutral)
>
> Write a 100 to 130 word email. Acknowledge the speculative approach positively — it took initiative. Be honest about {hiring_status} in one sentence. If there is a genuine adjacent opportunity or pipeline role, name it. If not, decline cleanly. Do not use "we'll be in touch" unless {hiring_status} indicates an active pipeline. Close with where they can monitor open roles at {company_name} (e.g. careers page, LinkedIn).

**When to use it:** When speculative inbound CVs pile up in the general inbox and need a response that respects the effort without creating false pipeline expectations.

**Pro tip:** If {hiring_status} is "potential pipeline for Q4," flag this candidate in your ATS immediately before sending — otherwise "we may be in touch" becomes an empty promise that damages trust when Q4 arrives and no one follows up.

> You are an HR manager writing a rejection email to a candidate referred by an internal employee who did not progress past the application review stage.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Internal referrer's name: {referrer_name}
> Relationship between referrer and candidate: {relationship} (e.g. former colleague, personal contact, university peer)
> Reason for rejection: {rejection_reason} (e.g. experience level below role requirement, skill set mismatch)
> Tone: warm and honest
>
> Write a 130 to 160 word rejection email addressed to the candidate. Acknowledge the referral from {referrer_name} without making it awkward. Decline clearly and state {rejection_reason} in one plain sentence — referred candidates often ask the referrer what happened, so vague language creates secondhand confusion. Do not ask the candidate to tell {referrer_name} — handle that separately. Close with genuine warmth.

**When to use it:** When a referred candidate didn't make the cut and the referrer is someone you'll see in the office tomorrow — a poorly worded rejection reflects on the internal relationship too.

**Pro tip:** Send a separate, brief Slack or email to {referrer_name} the same day so they hear it from you before the candidate tells them. Write that message separately — do not use this prompt for it.

> You are an HR manager writing a bulk rejection email to candidates who applied to a role that has been cancelled or put on hold before any interviews were conducted.
>
> Role that was cancelled: {job_title}
> Department: {department}
> Reason for cancellation (what you can share): {shareable_reason} (e.g. business restructuring, role scope under review, hiring freeze in the department)
> Tone: transparent / respectful
> Merge placeholder for first name: {first_name}
>
> Write a 90 to 120 word email using {{first_name}} as the merge field. Be honest that the role is on hold or cancelled using {shareable_reason} — candidates respect transparency and it prevents them wasting effort following up. Do not imply the role may reopen unless that is confirmed. Thank them for their time applying. Encourage them to monitor the careers page for future openings. Keep the close short.

**When to use it:** When a headcount freeze or reorg kills a live requisition and 80 applicants are sitting in the ATS expecting movement.

**Pro tip:** Do not write "we hope to revisit this role soon" if you don't know that to be true. Candidates who applied to a cancelled role are already frustrated — false hope followed by silence is worse than a clean close.

> You are an HR manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who applied for a graduate or entry-level role and showed potential but was not selected due to a competitive cohort.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role or programme: {programme_name} (e.g. 2026 Graduate Engineering Programme, Marketing Assistant role)
> One thing that stood out in their application: {application_highlight} (e.g. dissertation topic, extracurricular leadership, personal statement clarity)
> Reason for not selecting: competitive intake, not performance-based
> Future opportunity to flag: {future_opportunity} (e.g. next intake opens January 2027, junior analyst role opening in September)
> Tone: encouraging and specific
>
> Write a 160 to 200 word rejection email. Open by naming {application_highlight} — this person is early-career and specific feedback lands differently for them than for senior candidates. Make clear the decision was competitive, not a reflection of unsuitability. If {future_opportunity} exists, name it with enough detail that they can act on it. Do not use phrases like "keep developing yourself" without attaching something specific.

**When to use it:** After closing a graduate intake where 12 roles attracted 400 applicants and many strong candidates missed out purely on numbers.

**Pro tip:** For entry-level rejections, the email tone has outsized brand impact — these candidates become mid-market professionals in three to five years. A specific, respectful rejection is remembered. "Unfortunately on this occasion" is not.
