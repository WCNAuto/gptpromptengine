## Rejecting After a CV Screen (No Interview Took Place)

> You are an HR manager sending a post-CV-screen rejection email to a job applicant.
>
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Why they were not progressed: {reason — e.g. insufficient experience in X, overqualified, role filled internally}
> Tone: {warm and brief / professional and neutral / empathetic}
> Future consideration: {yes — keep on file / no}
>
> Write a 120 to 150 word rejection email. Open by thanking them for applying and naming the role. State clearly they have not been progressed without using vague language like "we've decided to move in a different direction." Give one honest, non-legal-risk sentence explaining why. If future consideration is yes, say so specifically. Close professionally. Do not use the phrase "we wish you all the best in your future endeavours."

**When to use it:** Monday morning when you have a stack of CV-screen rejects from the weekend's applications and need to clear them before the week's interviews begin.

**Pro tip:** If the real reason is "the hiring manager changed the brief after posting," do not put that in {reason}. Use "the role requirements shifted during the process" — it's honest and legally clean. Avoid putting competitor names or specific salary complaints in the variable field; ChatGPT will sometimes echo them verbatim into the email body.

> You are an HR manager writing a rejection email to a candidate whose CV showed strong general experience but not the specific technical skill the role requires.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Missing skill: {specific_skill — e.g. Salesforce CRM administration, SAP S/4HANA, ISO 27001 auditing}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Current hiring timeline: {actively hiring / pausing hiring after this role}
> Tone: {direct / encouraging}
>
> Write a 130 to 160 word rejection email. Name the specific skill gap clearly so the candidate understands exactly why they were not progressed. Do not soften it to the point of confusion. If the company is actively hiring, mention one other open role category they might suit. Close in under two sentences.

**When to use it:** When a candidate has a genuinely strong CV but a single hard-skill gap that disqualifies them — and you want to respect their time by being clear rather than vague.

**Pro tip:** Naming the missing skill feels risky but it dramatically reduces "can you give me feedback?" reply emails. Candidates who know exactly why they were rejected rarely follow up asking for more. Candidates who receive vague rejections almost always do.

> You are an HR manager rejecting a candidate who applied for a role that has since been filled internally before the external process concluded.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Stage reached: {CV screen only / one interview / two interviews}
> Time candidate has been waiting: {number_of_weeks}
> Tone: {apologetic but clear / neutral and professional}
>
> Write a 140 to 170 word rejection email. Acknowledge the wait explicitly if they have been waiting more than two weeks. Explain the role was filled internally without over-explaining or implying internal process failures. Do not ask them to "stay tuned for future opportunities" unless you are instructed to — instead, tell them how to monitor open roles on the careers page. Close with one sentence of genuine acknowledgement for their patience.

**When to use it:** When an internal promotion or lateral move closes a role mid-pipeline and you have external candidates still waiting for a decision.

**Pro tip:** Candidates who waited three or more weeks for a role filled internally are often frustrated enough to leave a Glassdoor review. A direct, un-hedged explanation ("the role was filled by an internal candidate") reduces that friction more than an apologetic but vague email does.

> You are an HR manager rejecting a candidate who applied speculatively — no role was advertised and no vacancy exists.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Type of role they were interested in: {function — e.g. finance, engineering, marketing}
> Talent pool / database: {yes, we will add them / no, we do not maintain one}
> Tone: {warm / neutral}
>
> Write a 100 to 130 word email acknowledging their speculative application. Be clear there is no current vacancy. If a talent pool exists, explain exactly what that means in practice — e.g. "we will contact you if a relevant role opens in the next 12 months." Do not promise contact you cannot deliver. Do not use the phrase "impressive background" unless their background was genuinely reviewed.

**When to use it:** When speculative LinkedIn messages or emailed CVs pile up in the shared recruitment inbox and you need a respectful but fast response.

**Pro tip:** If your company does not actually maintain a talent pool, do not say you'll "keep their CV on file." Candidates remember that promise and follow up six months later. Write "we do not hold CVs on file but encourage you to monitor our careers page" — it's cleaner for everyone.

> You are an HR manager rejecting a large batch of applicants who applied for a high-volume entry-level role. The rejection is post-CV-screen and none of these candidates were interviewed.
>
> Role: {job_title}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Number of applicants for context: {approximate_number — e.g. 200, 500}
> Key reason most were not progressed: {e.g. did not meet minimum qualification, role required specific licence, location mismatch}
> Tone: {professional and brief}
>
> Write a single 100 to 120 word rejection email suitable for bulk send. It must read as respectful and considered, not automated. Name the role. Give a one-sentence general reason for the high competition or minimum criterion. Do not personalise beyond the candidate name merge field — this must work as a mass email. Do not use exclamation marks.

**When to use it:** After closing applications on a high-volume role — graduate schemes, retail seasonal hires, customer service positions — where individual replies are not operationally possible but silence is unacceptable.

**Pro tip:** Run the output through your ATS merge-field syntax before bulk sending. ChatGPT will write `{candidate_name}` literally — you need to swap that for your ATS's own placeholder format (e.g. `[[FIRST_NAME]]` in Workday or `@firstname` in Greenhouse) before hitting send.

## Rejecting After a First Interview

> You are an HR manager writing a post-first-interview rejection email to a candidate.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Interview date: {interview_date}
> Main reason for rejection: {e.g. did not demonstrate enough leadership examples, communication style not suited to the team, another candidate was stronger on technical depth}
> Feedback policy: {we share brief feedback / we do not share feedback at this stage}
> Tone: {warm / professional}
>
> Write a 150 to 200 word rejection email. Open by thanking them for attending the interview on {interview_date} and for their preparation. If the feedback policy is "share brief feedback," give one specific, actionable sentence of feedback without making legal admissions. If the policy is "no feedback," decline politely without hiding behind "due to volume." Close by wishing them well in one sentence — specific, not boilerplate.

**When to use it:** The day after first-round interviews conclude and the shortlist has been confirmed by the hiring manager.

**Pro tip:** "Another candidate was stronger" is legally safer than "you lacked X skill" as a rejection reason in some jurisdictions — but it is also less useful to the candidate. Check your legal team's guidance before choosing what goes in {main_reason_for_rejection}. If you are in the UK, the Equality Act 2010 means documented, consistent feedback is protective, not risky.

> You are an HR manager rejecting a candidate after a first interview where the candidate was strong but the role was paused or cancelled entirely.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Reason role was paused or cancelled: {e.g. budget freeze, restructure, headcount reduction, strategic pivot}
> Whether you may rehire for this role: {likely in 6 months / unlikely / unknown}
> Tone: {transparent and empathetic / formal}
>
> Write a 160 to 190 word rejection email. Be explicit that the decision is about the role, not the candidate. Explain the business reason at the level of detail your company is comfortable sharing — use the {reason} directly. If there is a realistic chance of rehiring, say so with a timeframe. Do not invite them to "keep an eye on our website" as the only action — give them a specific contact or page URL if possible.

**When to use it:** When a hiring freeze or reorg kills a live role after you've already interviewed candidates who spent time preparing.

**Pro tip:** Candidates rejected due to a freeze are your warmest talent pool when headcount opens again. Add a note in your ATS tagging them as "paused — budget" rather than "rejected." When the role reopens, these people are pre-screened and already familiar with your company.

> You are an HR manager rejecting a candidate after a first interview where cultural fit was the concern, not skills.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Hiring manager's concern: {specific observable behaviour — e.g. talked over the interviewer repeatedly, gave answers focused entirely on individual output with no team examples, expressed a preference for fully autonomous work in a highly collaborative team}
> Company's working style: {e.g. collaborative and consensus-driven, fast-paced with high autonomy, structured with clear process adherence}
> Tone: {honest and direct / diplomatic}
>
> Write a 150 to 180 word rejection email. Do not use the phrase "culture fit" — it reads as a coded rejection and creates legal exposure. Instead, describe the mismatch in observable, role-specific terms using the hiring manager's concern as your source. Keep one sentence of genuine positive observation about the candidate. Do not invite them to re-apply without a clear timeline or changed circumstance.

**When to use it:** When the hiring manager's feedback is "they just wouldn't fit the team" and you need to translate that into a defensible, kind, and honest email.

**Pro tip:** Never copy the hiring manager's exact words into this prompt if they used protected-characteristic language ("she seemed too aggressive," "he seemed older in his thinking"). Describe only observable, role-relevant behaviour. If the HM's notes are problematic, do not use them as input at all — write the email based solely on what you observed.

> You are an HR manager writing a rejection email to an internal candidate who applied for a posted role and was interviewed but not selected.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Their current role: {current_job_title}
> Role they applied for: {applied_for_role}
> Manager who will also need to know: {line_manager_name — or "not applicable"}
> Main reason for non-selection: {e.g. the selected candidate had deeper specialist experience, the panel felt they needed one more year of leadership experience first}
> Next steps for the candidate: {e.g. development plan, another posted role, no immediate alternative}
> Tone: {empathetic and forward-looking}
>
> Write a 200 to 240 word rejection email to the internal candidate. Acknowledge the courage it takes to apply internally. Be specific about why they were not selected — internal candidates deserve more transparency than external ones. Reference the next step directly. Do not use corporate language like "this was a difficult decision" — state the reason clearly and move quickly to what happens next.

**When to use it:** When an internal applicant has been through the formal process and you need to communicate the outcome before word spreads informally through the team.

**Pro tip:** Send this before the successful candidate's offer is accepted and announced. Internal candidates who hear about a colleague's promotion through Slack before getting an official rejection email are far more likely to disengage or resign. Timing matters more than the words.

> You are an HR manager rejecting a candidate after a first interview where salary expectations were the deciding factor, not capability.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Salary expectation they stated: {candidate_salary_expectation}
> Salary band for the role: {role_salary_band}
> Gap: {e.g. £15,000 above band ceiling, 30% above budget}
> Whether the band may change: {yes — in the next budget cycle / no}
> Tone: {honest and respectful}
>
> Write a 130 to 160 word rejection email. Be direct that the primary reason is a salary mismatch, not a capability concern. State the band if your company policy permits it. If the band may change, say when and invite them to reapply at that point. Do not imply their expectations are unreasonable — acknowledge the market context. Close in two sentences.

**When to use it:** When a genuinely strong candidate is out of budget and you want to leave the door open without stringing them along.

**Pro tip:** If your company's pay transparency policy prohibits sharing the band, you can still say "the role's budget sits below your stated expectation" without giving a number. Vague rejections where salary was clearly the issue prompt follow-up negotiation emails — being direct closes that loop faster.

## Rejecting After a Final-Stage Interview or Assessment

> You are an HR manager writing a post-final-interview rejection email to a candidate who reached the last stage of a competitive process.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Stages they completed: {e.g. three interviews, a technical assessment, and a presentation to senior leadership}
> What made the winning candidate stronger: {one or two specific, factual differentiators — e.g. 8 years direct sector experience vs 3, demonstrated strategic budget ownership at scale}
> Feedback you are permitted to share: {yes / no}
> Tone: {warm, specific, and honest}
>
> Write a 220 to 270 word rejection email. Open by naming the stages they went through — it validates their effort. State clearly they were not selected. If feedback is permitted, give two specific sentences: one on a genuine strength observed, one on what the winning candidate brought that tipped the decision. Do not use "it was an incredibly tough decision" as a filler — if it genuinely was close, say what made it close. Close with a specific, genuine invitation to stay in contact if appropriate.

**When to use it:** After final-stage decisions are confirmed and the winning candidate has verbally accepted — do not send rejection emails to final-stage candidates before the offer is accepted.

**Pro tip:** Final-stage candidates who receive specific, honest feedback often become referrers, future applicants for different roles, or even clients. A generic rejection at this stage undoes all the goodwill built through the process. Spend the extra ninety seconds being specific — it pays back in employer brand.
