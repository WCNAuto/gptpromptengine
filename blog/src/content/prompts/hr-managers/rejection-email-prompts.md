## Post-Interview Rejection Emails

> You are an HR Manager writing a post-interview rejection email to a candidate who interviewed but will not be progressed.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role they applied for: {job_title}
> Interview stage reached: {interview_stage: phone screen / first round / second round / final round}
> One genuine strength you observed: {observed_strength}
> Reason for not progressing (internal only, do not state this verbatim): {internal_reason}
> Tone: {tone: warm / neutral / formal}
>
> Write a 150 to 200 word rejection email. Open by thanking them for their time at the {interview_stage}. Acknowledge one genuine strength without raising false hope. Decline clearly in the second paragraph — do not use passive constructions like "we have decided to move forward with other candidates" without stating they have not been selected. Close with one sentence wishing them well. Do not invite them to reapply unless explicitly told to. Do not mention the internal reason.

**When to use it:** After you've made a final hiring decision and need to clear your pending folder before the offer letter goes out.

**Pro tip:** If your internal reason is "culture fit," do not hint at it in any form. Courts in several jurisdictions have accepted vague rejection language as evidence of discriminatory intent when challenged. Keep the email on skills and role fit only.

> You are an HR Manager writing a post-final-round rejection email to a candidate who reached the last stage of a competitive hiring process.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Number of final-round candidates: {finalist_count}
> Specific thing they did well in the final interview: {final_round_strength}
> Hiring manager's name (optional, for sign-off): {hiring_manager_name}
> Tone: {tone: warm / formal}
>
> Write a 200 to 250 word email. Acknowledge that reaching the final round of {finalist_count} candidates is a real achievement. Be direct that they have not been selected. Do not say "we went with a stronger candidate" — frame it as a close decision based on role fit. Include one sentence of genuine recognition tied to {final_round_strength}. Close with a door-open line only if the tone is warm — something like noting they'd be welcome for future roles. Sign off from {hiring_manager_name} if provided.

**When to use it:** When a finalist has invested four or more hours across multiple interviews and deserves more than a three-line form email.

**Pro tip:** Final-round rejections are the most likely to generate a reply asking for feedback. If you're not prepared to give it, remove the door-open line — it signals feedback is available when it often isn't.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who interviewed well but is overqualified for the role.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role they applied for: {job_title}
> Their apparent seniority level: {candidate_seniority}
> What impressed you: {genuine_impression}
> Whether you want to keep them in your talent pipeline: {pipeline_yes_no: yes / no}
> Tone: {tone: warm / neutral}
>
> Write a 150 to 180 word rejection email. Be direct that they have not been selected. Do not use the word "overqualified" — frame it as a mismatch between the scope of the role and what they bring. If {pipeline_yes_no} is yes, include one sentence asking if they're open to being contacted when a more senior position opens. If no, close cleanly without that line.

**When to use it:** When a strong candidate applied for a role clearly beneath their experience level and you don't want to burn the relationship.

**Pro tip:** "Overqualified" is a phrase that can carry age-discrimination risk in some jurisdictions. This prompt avoids it by design — but also check that your ATS isn't logging "OQ" as a rejection reason without your knowledge.

> You are an HR Manager sending a rejection email to a candidate who performed well technically but whose salary expectations cannot be met.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Reason for not progressing (do not state verbatim): salary misalignment
> What the candidate did well: {technical_strength}
> Your company name: {company_name}
> Whether you want to revisit if budget changes: {revisit_option: yes / no}
>
> Write a 130 to 160 word email. Decline clearly. Do not disclose the budget ceiling or mention salary at all — keep the decline framed as a role-fit decision. If {revisit_option} is yes, include one sentence: "If our hiring situation changes, we'd like to stay in touch — would you be open to that?" If no, close without it. Tone: professional and respectful.

**When to use it:** When salary broke the deal in a negotiation and you've agreed internally not to disclose the figure.

**Pro tip:** If the candidate explicitly asked "was it salary?" in a previous email, you still don't have to confirm it here. Replying "we can't share specific decision factors" is sufficient and keeps you out of a back-and-forth that prolongs a closed process.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who reached second-round interview but had a skills gap that was a hard dealbreaker.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> The skills gap (internal only): {skills_gap}
> What they did well: {genuine_strength}
> Whether you want to suggest they upskill and reapply in future: {reapply_suggestion: yes / no}
> Tone: {tone: warm / neutral / formal}
>
> Write a 160 to 200 word rejection email. Thank them for their time in second-round interviews. Decline clearly. Do not name the skills gap explicitly — use a phrase like "the role requires a specific depth of experience in an area where we need someone who can contribute from day one." If {reapply_suggestion} is yes, include one sentence encouraging them to consider reapplying in 12 to 18 months as they develop in that area. Keep the close brief and genuine.

**When to use it:** When a candidate was clearly enthusiastic and close, but missing a technical requirement that wasn't negotiable.

**Pro tip:** If you choose the reapply line, note the candidate's name in your ATS with a "revisit" flag and the date — otherwise you'll reject them again in 14 months and the reapply suggestion will have been meaningless noise.

## High-Volume Application Rejection Emails

> You are an HR Manager sending a post-application rejection email to a candidate who applied but was screened out before any interview.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role they applied for: {job_title}
> Volume context (how many applied): {applicant_volume}
> Your company name: {company_name}
> Time between application and this email: {days_since_application} days
> Tone: {tone: warm / neutral / formal}
>
> Write a 100 to 130 word rejection email. Open by acknowledging the volume of applications received for {job_title} — briefly, not as an excuse. Thank them for their interest in {company_name}. Decline clearly without implying they did anything wrong. Do not offer feedback — at this stage none is available. Close with one line wishing them well in their search. Use first-person singular ("I") not "the team" or "we have decided."

**When to use it:** When you have 80-plus post-application rejections to send after a role closes and you need one output that doesn't sound like a mail-merge.

**Pro tip:** At the application stage, candidates haven't invested interview time — keep the email under 130 words. Longer emails at this stage read as performative, not kind. Brevity is the respectful move.

> You are an HR Manager sending a rejection email to a candidate who was screened out at the CV review stage because they did not meet a non-negotiable requirement.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Non-negotiable requirement they didn't meet (internal, do not name it): {missing_requirement}
> Your company name: {company_name}
> Whether the role had a right-to-work requirement that was the issue: {right_to_work_issue: yes / no}
>
> Write a 100 to 120 word rejection email. Decline clearly. If {right_to_work_issue} is yes, include one sentence: "This role requires the legal right to work in [country] without sponsorship, and we're unable to offer sponsorship at this time." If no, keep the decline generic: "After reviewing your application, we don't feel the role is the right match at this time." Close warmly with one sentence. Do not apologise more than once.

**When to use it:** When a hard eligibility filter eliminated a candidate and you need to decline without opening a feedback loop.

**Pro tip:** Right-to-work rejections need to be stated clearly — vague declines in this scenario can prompt candidates to email back asking if they can discuss options, which creates legal ambiguity around whether sponsorship was considered.

> You are an HR Manager sending a batch of rejection emails to candidates who applied for a role that has now been filled internally and was not opened to external candidates after all.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role they applied for: {job_title}
> Your company name: {company_name}
> Days since they applied: {days_since_application}
> Whether you will re-open the role externally in future: {will_reopen: yes / no}
>
> Write a 110 to 140 word rejection email explaining the situation honestly: the role was filled through an internal process and will not be progressed externally at this time. Apologise briefly for the time they invested in applying. If {will_reopen} is yes, note that the role or a similar one may be advertised again and they are welcome to apply. If no, close without that line. Tone: honest and respectful.

**When to use it:** After an internal hire closes a requisition that had active external applicants — often one of the most-delayed rejection communications in recruitment.

**Pro tip:** "Filled internally" is one of the few rejection reasons candidates find genuinely satisfying — it removes the sting of rejection as personal. Don't bury it in the second paragraph; lead with it.

> You are an HR Manager sending a rejection email to a candidate who applied for a role that has been put on hold or cancelled due to a hiring freeze.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role they applied for: {job_title}
> Your company name: {company_name}
> Whether the freeze is temporary or permanent: {freeze_status: temporary / permanent}
> Whether you want to keep their details on file: {keep_on_file: yes / no}
>
> Write a 120 to 150 word rejection email. Be honest that the role has been paused due to a change in business priorities — do not invent a skills reason. If {freeze_status} is temporary, say the role may reopen and you'll be in touch if it does. If permanent, thank them and close cleanly. If {keep_on_file} is yes, ask permission to retain their details for future opportunities. Tone: direct and respectful.

**When to use it:** When a hiring freeze hits mid-process and you need to close out applicant pipelines honestly.

**Pro tip:** If you ask to keep their details on file, you need a lawful basis to do so under data protection law (GDPR or equivalent). Make sure your ATS consent workflow is triggered before you retain anything — this email is not sufficient consent documentation on its own.

> You are an HR Manager sending a rejection email to a candidate who applied speculatively (no open role) and whose profile doesn't match anything in the pipeline.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Their speculative area of interest: {candidate_specialism}
> Your company name: {company_name}
> Whether you have a talent pool they can join: {talent_pool: yes / no}
> Tone: {tone: warm / neutral}
>
> Write a 100 to 130 word email acknowledging their speculative application. Explain there's no current opening that matches {candidate_specialism}. If {talent_pool} is yes, direct them to your talent pool or careers page and name it specifically. If no, decline cleanly. Do not promise to keep their CV on file unless your ATS actually does this. Tone: genuine, not corporate.

**When to use it:** When speculative applications land in your inbox and you've been letting them pile up unanswered.

**Pro tip:** Don't say "we'll keep your CV on file" as a kindness if you won't. Candidates follow up on that promise six months later, and your silence at that point damages your employer brand more than a clean decline today would have.

## Rejection After Assessment or Task Completion

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who completed a skills assessment or take-home task but will not be progressed.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Type of assessment completed: {assessment_type: coding test / written task / case study / personality profile}
> Time they likely invested: {time_invested: e.g. 2 hours / half a day}
> One honest thing that worked in their submission: {submission_strength}
> Tone: {tone: warm / neutral}
>
> Write a 180 to 220 word rejection email. Open by thanking them specifically for completing the {assessment_type} — name it, don't say "your submission." Acknowledge the time they invested. Decline clearly in the second paragraph. Include one sentence recognising {submission_strength} without implying they nearly made it. Do not offer detailed task feedback unless you intend to provide it. Close with a genuine good-luck line.

**When to use it:** After a test or task round closes and you've scored submissions — before moving the successful candidates to the next stage.

**Pro tip:** "We can't provide individual feedback on assessments" is a legally safe and honest line if your scoring rubric would reveal proprietary evaluation criteria. Add it as a single sentence if you're pre-empting the feedback request.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who completed a technical interview or live coding session and scored below the pass threshold.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Type of technical interview: {interview_type: live coding / system design / technical Q&A}
> Pass threshold (do not disclose): {pass_threshold}
> One area where they performed reasonably: {reasonable_area}
> Tone: {tone: warm / formal}
>
> Write a 160 to 200 word rejection email. Thank them for the {interview_type} session. Decline clearly — do not use "we were impressed but" as a lead-in if you weren't. Acknowledge {reasonable_area} briefly and genuinely. Do not disclose the scoring threshold or their score. If the tone is warm, close with one sentence encouraging them to consider applying again in future if the role reopens. Tone: honest and direct.

**When to use it:** After a scored technical interview round where multiple candidates fell below threshold on the same day.

**Pro tip:** If you scored them below 50% on a rubric, do not write "you performed well in several areas." Candidates who receive positive-sounding rejections and later learn their actual score report feeling misled — and they post about it publicly.
