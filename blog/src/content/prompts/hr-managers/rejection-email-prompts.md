## Early-Stage Rejections (Phone Screen and Application Review)

> You are an HR Manager sending a post-application rejection email to a candidate who applied online but was not selected for a phone screen.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Reason for rejection (internal, do not state verbatim): {rejection_reason: e.g. missing required qualification / overqualified / role paused}
> Tone: {warm_and_brief / formal / neutral}
> Hiring manager name (optional sign-off): {hiring_manager_name}
>
> Write a 100 to 130 word rejection email. Do not mention the internal rejection reason. Thank them for applying, decline clearly in sentence two, wish them well in sentence three. Do not use the phrase "we have decided to move forward with other candidates" — write something that sounds like a real person wrote it. Close with a single warm sentence, no hollow promises about "keeping their CV on file" unless that is genuinely true.

**When to use it:** Monday morning when you're clearing the weekend application queue and need to close out 15 to 30 applicants before the hiring manager asks for a pipeline update.

**Pro tip:** If the role is paused rather than filled, change {rejection_reason} to "role paused" and ask ChatGPT to leave the door open for reapplication in 90 days. A paused-role rejection that mentions a possible future opening gets significantly fewer reply complaints than a flat decline.

> You are an HR Manager rejecting a candidate after a CV review for a senior role where the candidate is clearly underqualified but has shown genuine enthusiasm.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Seniority level of role: {seniority: e.g. Director / Head of / Senior Manager}
> One thing that stood out positively on their CV: {positive_observation}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Tone: {encouraging / neutral}
>
> Write a 120 to 150 word rejection email. Acknowledge the specific positive observation in sentence two so it reads as personal, not generic. Be clear they are not progressing. If there is a junior or graduate version of this role at the company, suggest they look at it — only include this line if it feels natural, not as a consolation prize. No hollow flattery. End with a single sentence that is genuinely warm without being insincere.

**When to use it:** When a strong junior candidate has applied for a role two levels above them and you don't want to burn a future relationship by sending a form rejection.

**Pro tip:** The {positive_observation} variable does the heaviest lifting here. Pull one specific line from their CV — a project name, a metric, a company they've worked at — rather than a generic skill. ChatGPT will weave it in naturally and the email will read as handwritten.

> You are an HR Manager sending a rejection email to a candidate who applied for a role that has been put on hold internally, with no confirmed timeline for reopening.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Date the candidate applied: {application_date}
> Whether the company wants to keep them warm for future roles: {keep_warm: yes / no}
> Tone: {professional / conversational}
>
> Write a 110 to 140 word email. Do not say the role is cancelled — say it is on hold. If {keep_warm} is yes, include one honest sentence inviting them to connect on LinkedIn or check the careers page in 60 days. If {keep_warm} is no, close cleanly without that line. Do not promise the role will reopen. Do not apologise excessively.

**When to use it:** When a hiring freeze or budget cut hits mid-pipeline and you have 10 to 40 candidates who applied in good faith and need a response before they start chasing.

**Pro tip:** If you're sending this in bulk, add a line at the bottom of your ChatGPT prompt: "Generate five variations of the closing sentence so I can rotate them across a batch." That prevents identical emails landing in the same LinkedIn network.

> You are an HR Manager rejecting a high volume of candidates (20 or more) who applied via a job board for an entry-level role. The email must feel personal enough that it doesn't generate complaints but is short enough to send at scale.
>
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Industry: {industry}
> Tone: {professional / warm}
> One honest reason applications were not progressed (generic but true): {generic_true_reason: e.g. we received over 300 applications and prioritised candidates with direct industry experience}
>
> Write a 90 to 110 word rejection email with no candidate name (it will be mail-merged separately). It must not sound like an automated form. Include the {generic_true_reason} so the candidate understands the decision had context, not just "we found someone better." No hollow encouragement. End with one sentence that is respectful and final.

**When to use it:** After a high-volume job posting closes and your ATS rejection button produces something that reads like a tax notice.

**Pro tip:** The {generic_true_reason} is the single variable that separates a rejection candidates accept from one they reply to angrily. "We received 340 applications and prioritised direct sector experience" is specific enough to feel real, even in a mass email.

> You are an HR Manager declining a speculative or unsolicited job application from a candidate who reached out directly with no open role matching their background.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Candidate's background in one sentence: {candidate_background}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Whether there is a careers page or talent pool they can join: {talent_pool_link_or_no}
> Tone: {brief_and_warm / formal}
>
> Write an 80 to 100 word response. Thank them for reaching out directly — that initiative is worth noting. Decline clearly without inventing a role that doesn't exist. If {talent_pool_link_or_no} contains a link, include it naturally in the second-to-last sentence. Do not say "we'll keep your details on file" if you won't. Close with one sentence that respects their time.

**When to use it:** When your LinkedIn inbox has three to eight unsolicited CVs from the past two weeks and you need to close them politely without ignoring them or leading anyone on.

**Pro tip:** Speculative applicants are often the most proactive candidates in your future talent pool. If {talent_pool_link_or_no} links to a real talent community, the conversion rate on those links from personalised rejections is noticeably higher than from career page banners.

## Post-Interview Rejections (First and Second Round)

> You are an HR Manager sending a rejection email after a first-round interview. The candidate interviewed well but was not selected for a second round due to a skills gap identified during the interview.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Specific skill or area where the gap was identified (internal framing): {skills_gap}
> Whether feedback is being offered: {offer_feedback: yes / no}
> Tone: {warm_and_direct / formal}
>
> Write a 150 to 180 word rejection email. Thank them for their time in the first round. Decline clearly in paragraph one. In paragraph two, if {offer_feedback} is yes, invite them to request a brief feedback call — do not deliver the feedback in the email itself. If {offer_feedback} is no, close with a positive but honest sentence. Do not mention the {skills_gap} by name in the email. Do not use the phrase "not the right fit."

**When to use it:** After first-round debrief when the hiring manager has given you a reason but you're not ready to put it in writing.

**Pro tip:** Never write the actual skills gap into a rejection email — even if {offer_feedback} is yes, keep it to a verbal call. Written feedback citing a specific gap creates a paper trail that has been used in discrimination complaints even when the original decision was entirely legitimate.

> You are an HR Manager rejecting a candidate after a second-round panel interview for a mid-level role. The candidate was a finalist but the panel selected another candidate. The hiring decision was close.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Seniority of role: {seniority_level}
> One genuine strength the candidate showed during interview: {interview_strength}
> Tone: {warm / professional}
> Whether to offer to keep them in mind for future roles: {future_consideration: yes / no}
>
> Write a 180 to 220 word rejection email. Open by acknowledging the time they invested in the process — second-round candidates have committed significant effort. Name the {interview_strength} genuinely in paragraph two so it reads as personal. Be clear the decision has been made. If {future_consideration} is yes, include one specific and honest sentence about why you would consider them again — not a generic "we'll keep you on file." Close with a sentence that respects the disappointment without over-apologising.

**When to use it:** After a close final-round decision where the hiring manager says "it could have gone either way" — the candidate deserves better than a one-paragraph form rejection.

**Pro tip:** If you genuinely want to keep this candidate warm, add their name to a separate tracker before you send the email. The email means nothing if you don't follow up in three to six months. ChatGPT can draft that follow-up email too — prompt for it separately now while the context is fresh.

> You are an HR Manager rejecting a candidate after a technical assessment or skills test that they did not pass at the required standard.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Type of assessment: {assessment_type: e.g. coding test / writing sample / data analysis task}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Whether the company will share assessment results: {share_results: yes / no}
> Tone: {direct / warm}
>
> Write a 140 to 170 word rejection email. Acknowledge they completed the {assessment_type} — that effort deserves recognition. Decline clearly. If {share_results} is yes, include one sentence offering to share their results on request. If {share_results} is no, do not reference the results at all. Do not say they "didn't quite meet the bar" — write something more specific to the situation. Close with one sentence that is respectful and final.

**When to use it:** After assessment results come back below the pass threshold and you need to close the candidate the same day, not two weeks later when they're already following up.

**Pro tip:** If your assessment is timed, candidates often know how they performed. A vague rejection after a test they struggled with reads as dishonest. Being specific that the role requires a score threshold (without giving the number) is more credible and generates fewer reply emails asking "why."

> You are an HR Manager sending a post-interview rejection to a candidate who was interviewed for a role that has since been filled internally rather than externally.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Whether to explain the internal hire reason: {explain_reason: yes / no}
> Date of their interview: {interview_date}
> Tone: {transparent / formal}
>
> Write a 130 to 160 word rejection email. If {explain_reason} is yes, include one clear sentence explaining the role was filled by an internal candidate — this is honest and candidates generally accept it. If {explain_reason} is no, decline without giving a reason. Do not apologise repeatedly. Do not suggest they reapply if you have no open roles. Close with a warm but final sentence.

**When to use it:** When a role is filled internally after external candidates have already been through one or more interview rounds and you have been putting off the call because the conversation feels awkward.

**Pro tip:** "The role was filled by an internal candidate" is one of the most accepted rejection reasons candidates receive. Candidates who get this explanation reply far less frequently than those who receive a vague "we've decided to move in a different direction." Use it when it's true.

>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Genuine positive from the interview: {genuine_positive}
> Whether to offer feedback: {offer_feedback: yes / no}
> Tone: {professional / warm}
>
> Write a 140 to 160 word rejection email. Do not reference communication, presentation, articulation, or any soft skill as the reason for the decision. Name {genuine_positive} to make the email feel personal. Decline clearly. If {offer_feedback} is yes, invite a feedback call — keep it verbal. Close with one sentence that is respectful. The email must read as empathetic without being patronising.

**When to use it:** When the debrief note reads "great experience but didn't come across well on camera" and you need to close the candidate professionally without creating a discrimination risk.

**Pro tip:** Communication-style rejections are a legal grey area in several jurisdictions. Never put the real reason in writing. If the candidate pushes back in a reply, route it to legal or HR leadership before responding — do not engage in a back-and-forth over email.

## Final-Round and Offer-Stage Rejections

> You are an HR Manager sending a rejection email to a candidate who reached final round and was runner-up after a competitive hiring process for a senior role.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Seniority: {seniority_level}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Length of interview process: {process_length: e.g. four rounds over six weeks}
> Whether to offer a call with the hiring manager: {call_offer: yes / no}
> Tone: {warm_and_direct / professional}
>
> Write a 220 to 280 word rejection email. This candidate has invested significant time and deserves a substantive response. Open by naming the length of the process — {process_length} — to acknowledge their commitment. Be clear the decision has been made in paragraph one. Use paragraph two to name one genuine quality they demonstrated across the process, without being vague. If {call_offer} is yes, offer a specific call with the hiring manager — not a generic "feel free to reach out." Close with a paragraph that is warm, honest, and final. Do not use the phrase "we were blown away" or "incredibly impressed."

**When to use it:** After a four to six round senior process where the runner-up candidate has given references, met the CEO, or completed a paid assessment — anything that required real commitment.

**Pro tip:** If {call_offer} is yes, book the call before the email goes out. Final-round candidates who receive an offer of a call and then get no response when they take you up on it remember it for years. Hiring reputations at the senior level travel fast.
