## Post-Interview Rejection Emails

> You are an HR Manager writing a post-interview rejection email to a candidate.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Interview stage reached: {interview_stage: phone screen / first round / second round / final round}
> Primary reason for not progressing (internal only, do not state directly): {rejection_reason}
> One genuine strength to acknowledge: {candidate_strength}
> Hiring manager name: {hiring_manager_name}
> Tone: {tone: warm / professional / brief}
>
> Write a 150 to 200 word rejection email. Open by thanking the candidate for their time at the {interview_stage}. Acknowledge their {candidate_strength} in one specific sentence without overpromising a future. Deliver the decline clearly in the second paragraph — no passive voice, no "we have decided to move forward with other candidates" clichés. Close by wishing them well genuinely. Sign off from {hiring_manager_name}. Do not invite them to reapply unless explicitly instructed.

**When to use it:** The morning after a final-round decision when you owe a personal response to someone who interviewed twice or more.

**Pro tip:** If the {rejection_reason} is "culture fit," replace it with the actual observed gap before pasting — "culture fit" fed into the prompt produces a vague email that reads as dismissive.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email after a technical assessment or take-home task.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Task completed: {task_description}
> Time the candidate invested: {time_invested: e.g. "a 3-hour coding task" or "a full-day case study"}
> Specific element that was strong: {strong_element}
> Gap that led to the decision: {deciding_gap}
> Tone: {tone: direct / empathetic}
>
> Write a 180 to 220 word rejection email. Acknowledge the {time_invested} explicitly in sentence one — name the effort, not just the task. In the body, reference {strong_element} with one concrete observation. State the outcome clearly. You may briefly name {deciding_gap} at a high level if it helps the candidate grow — frame it as context, not criticism. Close with a specific good wish tied to their career, not a generic "best of luck." Do not offer feedback calls unless {tone} is empathetic and word count allows.

**When to use it:** After a take-home task round when you want to respect the candidate's time investment without triggering a lengthy feedback exchange.

**Pro tip:** Naming the {time_invested} explicitly ("we know the case study took most of a day") increases the chance the candidate responds positively and refers others — worth the one extra sentence.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who reached the final round but was pipped by one other applicant.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Department: {department}
> What made this a close decision: {close_decision_note: e.g. "two candidates were nearly identical on technical skill; the other had three years of direct sector experience"}
> Specific strength of this candidate: {candidate_strength}
> Whether to keep them in talent pool: {talent_pool: yes / no}
> Tone: {tone: warm / candid}
>
> Write a 200 to 250 word rejection email. Be honest that this was a close decision without being performative about it. Name {candidate_strength} once, specifically. If {talent_pool} is yes, include a clear sentence inviting them to stay in contact for future {department} roles — make it sound like a real intention, not a consolation prize. If {talent_pool} is no, omit that section entirely. Do not use the phrase "we were blown away" or "you were an exceptional candidate."

**When to use it:** When a strong finalist lost to a marginally stronger hire and you genuinely want to keep the door open — or when you need to close the loop cleanly without false hope.

**Pro tip:** If {talent_pool} is yes, set a calendar reminder for 90 days to actually re-contact this person. The email means nothing without the follow-through.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who was referred internally by an employee.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Internal referrer name: {referrer_name}
> Relationship between referrer and candidate: {relationship: e.g. "former colleague", "university friend"}
> Primary reason for declining: {decline_reason_internal}
> Whether the referrer has already been informed: {referrer_informed: yes / no}
> Tone: {tone: warm / professional}
>
> Write a 160 to 200 word rejection email. Acknowledge the referral connection naturally without making it the centrepiece. Decline clearly and respectfully. Do not reference {decline_reason_internal} directly in the email. If {referrer_informed} is no, add one sentence noting that {referrer_name} will hear from the team separately, so the candidate is not blindsided. Do not frame the rejection as the referrer's fault. Close warmly.

**When to use it:** When a referral hire doesn't work out and you need to manage the candidate's exit without damaging the referring employee's relationship with HR.

**Pro tip:** Always send this email before or at the same time as you speak to the referrer — candidates who hear the bad news from the referrer first feel ambushed, which creates a retention risk on your team.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who applied for a senior leadership role.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title: e.g. "VP of Operations", "Head of Finance"}
> Whether they had a formal interview: {interviewed: yes / no}
> One credible observation about their background: {credible_observation}
> Reason for decline (internal): {decline_reason}
> Seniority of sign-off: {signatory_name} and {signatory_title}
>
> Write a 180 to 220 word rejection email. Match the register to the seniority of the role — no casual language, no filler phrases. If {interviewed} is yes, reference the conversation briefly. Acknowledge {credible_observation} in one measured sentence. Decline directly in the second paragraph. Close with a sentence that treats the candidate as a professional peer. Sign off from {signatory_name}, {signatory_title}. Do not include a feedback offer unless explicitly instructed.

**When to use it:** When rejecting a director-level or C-suite candidate who expects communication that matches their seniority — not a generic HR form letter.

**Pro tip:** Senior candidates often know the hiring team personally or will encounter them at industry events. A vague or clumsy email at this level causes reputational damage. Keep the tone peer-to-peer, not HR-to-applicant.

## High-Volume Application Rejection Emails

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who applied online and did not reach the interview stage.
>
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Business reason applications are closed or role is filled: {closure_reason: role filled / volume exceeded / position paused}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Whether future roles are realistic: {future_roles: yes / no}
> Tone: {tone: warm / neutral / brief}
>
> Write a 100 to 130 word rejection email addressed to "Hi [First Name]" (note to user: your ATS will personalise this field). Thank them for applying to {company_name}. State clearly that they have not been shortlisted for {job_title}. Give the {closure_reason} in plain language. If {future_roles} is yes, include one sentence directing them to the careers page. If no, omit. Do not apologise excessively. Do not promise to keep their CV on file unless that is actually true. Sign off with the company name, not a named individual.

**When to use it:** For ATS-triggered rejections going out to 50 or more applicants after a role closes — where personalisation isn't feasible but the email still needs to sound human.

**Pro tip:** "We will keep your CV on file" in a high-volume email is almost never true and candidates know it. Remove it. It does more reputational damage than saying nothing.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate whose application was screened out due to a missing essential requirement.
>
> Candidate name (or merge field): {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Missing requirement: {missing_requirement: e.g. "right to work in the UK without sponsorship", "minimum five years in a regulated environment"}
> Whether sponsorship or exceptions are possible now or in future: {exception_possible: yes / no}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Tone: {tone: clear / empathetic}
>
> Write a 120 to 150 word rejection email. State up front that {candidate_name}'s application for {job_title} cannot progress. Name {missing_requirement} plainly — do not bury it. If {exception_possible} is yes, include one sentence explaining what would need to change. If no, say so directly so the candidate does not reapply unnecessarily. Do not apologise for the requirement existing. Close briefly and professionally.

**When to use it:** When a candidate is being declined for a hard eligibility reason — visa, licence, qualification — and you want to save them and yourself the back-and-forth of an ambiguous email.

**Pro tip:** Naming the specific {missing_requirement} in plain language reduces reply volume by roughly half compared to emails that say "your application does not meet our current criteria." Candidates who know the exact reason don't follow up asking why.

> You are an HR Manager writing a batch rejection email to candidates who applied after a role was already filled internally.
>
> Role title: {job_title}
> Department: {department}
> How long the role was posted externally before being filled internally: {posting_duration: e.g. "two weeks"}
> Whether similar roles come up regularly: {similar_roles_frequency: often / occasionally / rarely}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Tone: {tone: brief / warm}
>
> Write a 110 to 140 word rejection email. Explain clearly that {job_title} in {department} has been filled through an internal appointment and the external process is now closed. Do not imply the candidate was screened or found lacking — this is a process outcome, not an assessment. If {similar_roles_frequency} is often or occasionally, include one sentence directing them to the {company_name} careers page. If rarely, omit. Keep the close brief. Sign off with the company name.

**When to use it:** When a requisition was posted externally out of process obligation but a strong internal candidate was always the likely hire — and now you owe 40 applicants an honest explanation.

**Pro tip:** Do not say "we received an overwhelming number of applications" when the role was filled internally. Candidates find this dishonest and it surfaces in employer review sites.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who applied for a role that has been put on hold or cancelled.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Reason the role is paused or cancelled: {pause_reason: budget freeze / restructure / headcount reduction / strategic reprioritisation}
> Whether the role may reopen: {may_reopen: likely / possible / unlikely}
> Interview stage the candidate reached: {stage_reached: applied / phone screen / first interview / further}
> Tone: {tone: transparent / professional}
>
> Write a 140 to 180 word rejection email. Be honest that the role has been paused or cancelled and give {pause_reason} in plain language — not corporate euphemism. If {stage_reached} is first interview or further, acknowledge their time specifically. If {may_reopen} is likely or possible, include one sentence saying you will contact them if the role reopens, and mean it. If unlikely, say so and close cleanly. Do not apologise beyond one brief acknowledgement.

**When to use it:** When a headcount freeze or restructure kills a live requisition mid-hiring process and you need to notify candidates honestly without triggering legal or PR risk.

**Pro tip:** "Strategic reprioritisation" reads as evasive to candidates. Use it internally but translate it to plain language in the email — "a budget decision this quarter" is more honest and generates less backlash.

> You are an HR Manager writing a brief, neutral rejection email to a candidate whose CV was not shortlisted due to overall fit but who may be right for future roles.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Reason not shortlisted (internal note only): {internal_reason}
> Whether you want to proactively add them to a talent pool: {add_to_talent_pool: yes / no}
> Future role type they might suit: {future_role_type}
> Company name: {company_name}
>
> Write a 120 to 150 word rejection email. Decline clearly without referencing {internal_reason}. If {add_to_talent_pool} is yes, include two sentences: one explaining that their profile has been noted for {future_role_type} opportunities, and one telling them what happens next (e.g. they may be contacted in future). If no, omit entirely and close cleanly. Do not use the phrase "you were not the right fit at this time."

**When to use it:** When you're managing a pipeline and want to preserve strong CVs for upcoming requisitions without overpromising or clogging the candidate's inbox with false hope.

**Pro tip:** If {add_to_talent_pool} is yes, tag the candidate in your ATS immediately after sending — not later. The email creates an expectation. The tag makes you able to fulfil it.

## Rejection Emails for Specific Situations

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who behaved unprofessionally during the hiring process but does not need to be told that directly.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Unprofessional behaviour observed (internal, do not reference): {behaviour_observed: e.g. "no-showed first interview", "sent aggressive follow-up emails", "badmouthed previous employer in detail"}
> Stage reached before decision: {stage_reached}
> Tone: {tone: neutral / brief / professional}
>
> Write a 100 to 130 word rejection email. Decline clearly and politely. Do not reference {behaviour_observed} under any circumstances. Do not give feedback. Do not invite reapplication or future contact. Keep every sentence factual and neutral. Close with a short, professional good wish. This email should be legally clean and emotionally flat — nothing that could be forwarded with a complaint attached.

**When to use it:** When a candidate has been difficult, aggressive, or unprofessional and you need to close the process without creating a paper trail that invites a grievance.

**Pro tip:** Forward this email to your legal or ER team before sending if the behaviour was serious (harassment, threats). "Legally clean and emotionally flat" is a standard, not a guarantee — don't handle hostile candidates in isolation.
