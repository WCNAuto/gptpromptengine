## Post-Phone-Screen Rejections

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who did not pass a phone screening.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Specific reason for rejection (internal only — do NOT include this verbatim in the email): {internal_reason}
> Tone: {warm_and_brief / professional_and_direct}
>
> Write a 120 to 150 word rejection email. Open by thanking {candidate_name} for their time. In one sentence, communicate that they will not be moving forward without explaining the internal reason. Wish them well in their search. Do not invite them to reapply unless instructed. Do not use the phrases "we have decided to move in a different direction" or "we will keep your CV on file" — find a more direct alternative. Sign off from HR at {company_name}.

**When to use it:** Monday morning after a Friday phone screen batch. You have six rejections to send before your 10am stand-up.

**Pro tip:** Fill in {internal_reason} honestly (e.g. "salary expectation £20k above band") but do not paste it into the email — it anchors the AI to write around that gap without revealing it.

> You are an HR Manager writing a high-volume phone screen rejection email to be sent to multiple candidates in one hiring round.
>
> Role: {job_title}
> Department: {department}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Number of applicants screened this round: {number_screened}
> Key reason most candidates were screened out: {common_gap — e.g. "no experience with [specific tool]"}
> Tone: {efficient_and_respectful}
>
> Write a 100 to 130 word rejection email suitable for sending to all screened-out candidates from this round. It must feel personal enough not to read as a mail-merge blast, so include one specific sentence acknowledging the competitive applicant pool for {job_title}. Do not mention {common_gap} by name. Close by wishing the candidate well. No invitation to reapply.

**When to use it:** When you screened 40 people for one role and need a single email that doesn't feel like an automated reject.

**Pro tip:** Add the candidate's first name as a mail-merge field before sending so the AI's "personal touch" language isn't undercut by a generic salutation.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who was screened out because the role's requirements changed after the job was posted.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Company name: {company_name}
> How the role changed: {brief_description — e.g. "now requires team management experience"}
> Tone: {apologetic_but_clear}
>
> Write a 150 to 180 word rejection email. Acknowledge that {candidate_name} invested time in the process. Explain briefly that the role requirements have evolved without over-explaining or making the company sound disorganised. Do not promise to reconsider the CV for the changed role. Close warmly.

**When to use it:** When a hiring manager changes the spec mid-search and you have candidates already in the funnel who no longer fit.

**Pro tip:** Do not let the AI say "we are evolving as an organisation" — it reads as corporate spin. If the output includes it, add the instruction "remove all references to organisational evolution" in a follow-up message.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who was screened out due to location or remote work policy.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Work arrangement for this role: {fully_onsite / hybrid_X_days / specific_location}
> Candidate's stated location or preference: {candidate_location_or_preference}
> Tone: {direct_and_kind}
>
> Write a 120 to 140 word rejection email. State clearly that the role requires {work_arrangement} and that this is the reason they are not progressing. Do not soften it to the point where the candidate is left uncertain about whether they could negotiate. Close by encouraging them to watch for future remote roles if applicable.

**When to use it:** When a candidate listed themselves as fully remote and the role requires three days onsite — and you want to close the loop without ambiguity.

**Pro tip:** Clarity here protects you from a candidate emailing back asking "would you consider flexible arrangements?" — which costs everyone more time. Make the AI's language unambiguous on the first pass.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who applied but was never contacted because the role was paused or cancelled.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Reason role was paused: {budget_freeze / restructure / headcount_change / hiring_pause}
> Time since application: {weeks_or_months_since_they_applied}
> Tone: {apologetic_and_honest}
>
> Write a 140 to 160 word rejection email. Acknowledge the delay explicitly — {candidate_name} has been waiting {time_since_application} and deserves an honest explanation. State that the role is paused or cancelled due to {reason_role_paused} without making it sound like a personal rejection. Invite them to reapply when future roles open. Sign off from HR at {company_name}.

**When to use it:** When you're clearing a backlog of ghosted applicants from a hiring pause and need to close the loop at scale.

**Pro tip:** If {time_since_application} is more than three months, add a line of acknowledgement — "we understand this is a long wait and we appreciate your patience." Without it, the email reads as tone-deaf regardless of how polished the rest is.

## Post-First-Interview Rejections

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate after a first-round interview.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Interviewer(s): {interviewer_names}
> Interview date: {interview_date}
> General reason for not progressing (do NOT quote verbatim in email): {internal_reason}
> Tone: {warm / neutral / formal}
>
> Write a 170 to 210 word rejection email. Thank {candidate_name} by name for meeting with {interviewer_names} on {interview_date}. Communicate that they will not be progressing without naming the internal reason. Include one specific, genuine sentence of positive feedback that reflects that a real interview happened — do not use generic phrases like "we were impressed by your background." Wish them well. Do not invite reapplication unless the variable below is set to yes.
> Invite reapplication: {yes / no}

**When to use it:** After a first-round debrief where the hiring panel has decided not to progress but you want the candidate to feel their time was seen.

**Pro tip:** The "genuine positive feedback" instruction is the hardest for the AI to execute without more context. Add one bullet of real feedback from your interview notes (e.g. "strong clarity on stakeholder management examples") to the prompt before running it — the output will be meaningfully more specific.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who interviewed well but was beaten by a stronger applicant.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Company name: {company_name}
> What made the successful candidate stronger (internal only): {differentiating_factor}
> Will this role or a similar role open again soon: {yes_timeframe / no / unknown}
> Tone: {honest_and_encouraging}
>
> Write a 180 to 220 word rejection email. Do not say "we went with someone with more experience" — find a phrasing that's honest without inviting a debate. Acknowledge that {candidate_name} performed well. If {will_role_open_again} is yes or unknown, include a genuine invitation to stay in touch for future roles. If no, do not include false hope. Close warmly.

**When to use it:** When it's a genuine numbers game and you want to leave the door open without saying things you can't back up.

**Pro tip:** The phrase "we had a very competitive pool" is technically true but candidates have heard it thousands of times. Prompt the AI to avoid it, or add "do not use the phrase 'competitive pool'" as a constraint — the alternatives it generates are almost always better.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who was overqualified for the role.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Candidate's seniority level based on CV/interview: {seniority_level — e.g. "Director-level"}
> Actual seniority of the role: {role_level — e.g. "Senior Manager"}
> Tone: {respectful_and_direct}
>
> Write a 160 to 200 word rejection email. Be honest that the scope of {job_title} at {company_name} is unlikely to match {candidate_name}'s experience level, and that this is the primary reason for not progressing — not a skills gap. Avoid patronising language. Do not suggest they would be bored — frame it around role fit and scope alignment. If there is a more senior role that might be relevant, you may mention it — otherwise do not invent one.

**When to use it:** When a candidate has applied down — perhaps between roles — and you've decided to reject rather than risk attrition in the first six months.

**Pro tip:** Overqualification rejections carry legal risk if they are perceived as age-related. Review the AI's output for any language that references career stage, years of experience, or seniority in a way that could imply age. Remove it before sending.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email after a first interview where the candidate's salary expectation is significantly above the role's band.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Salary band for this role: {salary_range}
> Candidate's stated expectation: {candidate_expectation}
> Tone: {clear_and_respectful}
>
> Write a 150 to 180 word rejection email. State clearly and early that the role's compensation is {salary_range} and that this represents a gap from {candidate_name}'s expectation. Do not apologise excessively. Do not suggest the band might flex unless it actually can. If the band is firm, say so cleanly. Invite {candidate_name} to reach out if their situation changes or if they wish to discuss roles at a different level. Close without false warmth.

**When to use it:** When salary was discussed in the screening call and the gap only became fully clear post-interview — you need to close this without leaving the candidate guessing.

**Pro tip:** Include the actual salary range in the email rather than directing the AI to be vague. Candidates who know the number can make an informed decision; those left guessing are more likely to follow up and consume more of your time.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who interviewed for a role that has since been filled internally.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Interview date: {interview_date}
> Internal candidate's relationship to the role: {e.g. "acting in the role" / "internal promotion"}
> Tone: {transparent_and_appreciative}
>
> Write a 160 to 190 word rejection email. Acknowledge that {candidate_name} went through the interview process in good faith. Explain that the role has been filled internally, and that this decision was not a reflection of their interview performance. Do not fabricate feedback. Close by encouraging them to apply for future external openings at {company_name}.

**When to use it:** When an internal hire was always likely but the role was posted externally, and the external candidate deserves an honest explanation.

**Pro tip:** Avoid the AI defaulting to "we are committed to internal development" — it sounds like the company is justifying itself to the candidate rather than informing them. If it appears, rerun with the instruction to remove any company self-promotion.

## Post-Final-Interview and Offer-Stage Rejections

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a finalist candidate who made it to the final round but was not selected.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Number of finalists in the round: {number_of_finalists}
> Specific feedback you are authorised to share: {one_or_two_sentences_of_genuine_feedback}
> Tone: {warm_and_substantive}
>
> Write a 220 to 270 word rejection email. Acknowledge that reaching the final round of {number_of_finalists} candidates represents a real achievement. Include the authorised feedback from {specific_feedback} — do not paraphrase it into vagueness. Do not invent feedback the HR Manager has not provided. Communicate clearly that they were not selected. Invite them to stay in touch for future roles. This is a candidate worth keeping in the talent pool — the tone should reflect that without making promises.

**When to use it:** After a final debrief where the decision was close and you want to retain the runner-up's goodwill for a future hire.

**Pro tip:** "Stay in touch" is meaningless unless you act on it. If you genuinely want this candidate in your pipeline, CC yourself on the email as a reminder to tag them in your ATS before the end of the day.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who completed a paid work trial or paid assessment day and was not selected.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Duration and format of the work trial: {trial_details — e.g. "two-day paid trial in the finance team"}
> Payment status: {already_paid / payment_being_processed}
> Tone: {grateful_and_clear}
>
> Write a 180 to 220 word rejection email. Acknowledge the significant time {candidate_name} invested in the {trial_details}. Confirm payment status clearly — {payment_status}. Communicate that they will not be progressing. Offer one or two sentences of genuine appreciation without overpromising on feedback unless a debrief is planned. Do not pad the email with platitudes to compensate for the difficult news. Close professionally.

**When to use it:** After a hands-on trial or assessment centre where candidates gave full working days and deserve a rejection that acknowledges that investment.

**Pro tip:** If payment is still being processed, name the expected date in the email. Ambiguity on pay creates follow-up emails and, occasionally, complaints. One specific sentence ("your payment will be processed by {payment_date}") removes that entirely.
