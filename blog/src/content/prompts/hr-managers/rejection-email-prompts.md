## Post-Interview Rejection Emails

> You are an HR Manager writing a post-interview rejection email to a candidate who reached the final or second round but was not selected.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Interview stage they reached: {interview_stage}
> Primary reason not selected (internal only, paraphrase sensitively): {rejection_reason}
> One genuine strength you observed: {candidate_strength}
> Whether to leave the door open for future roles: {yes / no}
> Tone: {warm / neutral / formal}
>
> Write a 150 to 200 word rejection email. Open by thanking them for their time at the {interview_stage} stage. Name the {candidate_strength} sincerely — one sentence, specific. Deliver the decision clearly in the second paragraph without burying it. If {yes}, close with a genuine invitation to apply again. Do not use phrases like "we had many strong candidates" or "we wish you all the best in your future endeavours."

**When to use it:** Monday morning after a Friday panel interview, when you have four finalists and only one offer to make.

**Pro tip:** If the rejection reason is something fixable — like a skills gap — you can name it in the prompt as the strength angle ("showed strong potential but needs X"). ChatGPT will frame it as developmental rather than disqualifying, which is more useful feedback for the candidate and protects you from awkward follow-up calls.

> You are an HR Manager sending a rejection email after a first-round phone screen.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Department: {department}
> Reason for not progressing (internal): {screening_gap}
> Whether the candidate seemed enthusiastic or prepared: {candidate_impression: enthusiastic / unprepared / professional}
> Tone: {brief and respectful / warm / formal}
>
> Write a 100 to 130 word rejection email. Keep it short — this was an early-stage screen, not a deep investment. Open with thanks. Confirm you won't be progressing at this time. Do not explain the {screening_gap} in detail. Close with one courteous sentence. No hollow encouragement. No promises about keeping their CV on file unless you actually will.

**When to use it:** When you screened 20 people in one day and need to close out the ones who didn't make the shortlist before end of week.

**Pro tip:** If your ATS sends automated rejections, use this for the candidates you personally spoke to. The personal touch matters most where there was a real conversation. Automated rejections for unscreened applicants are fine; this prompt is for the ones who gave you 20 minutes.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who interviewed well but was pipped by an internal candidate for the role.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Hiring outcome: internal candidate selected
> Number of interview rounds the external candidate completed: {rounds_completed}
> One specific area where they impressed: {standout_quality}
> Whether to invite them to a talent pool: {yes / no}
> Tone: {warm and honest / neutral}
>
> Write a 160 to 200 word rejection email. Be transparent that the decision came down to an internal appointment, not a failing on their part — one clear sentence. Acknowledge the {rounds_completed} rounds they invested. Name the {standout_quality} genuinely. If {yes}, explain the talent pool invitation with one concrete sentence about what it means in practice. Do not make the internal hire sound like a consolation story.

**When to use it:** When an internal promotion or sideways move closes a role mid-process — common in restructures and succession-planning cycles.

**Pro tip:** Candidates who lose to internal hires are among the most likely to reapply or refer others if you treat them well. The one concrete sentence about what the talent pool "means in practice" matters — something like "we have two similar roles opening in Q1" is worth far more than "we'll keep your details on file."

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a senior candidate (director or VP level) who reached the final stage of a competitive executive search.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role title: {job_title}
> Stage reached: {final_round_detail}
> What made the decision close: {deciding_factor}
> One leadership quality they demonstrated: {leadership_strength}
> Hiring Manager name signing off: {hiring_manager_name}
> Tone: {executive / direct / respectful}
>
> Write a 200 to 250 word rejection email written from {hiring_manager_name}. Open with genuine respect for their engagement at the {final_round_detail} level. Be clear the decision was close and name the {deciding_factor} honestly but diplomatically. Acknowledge the {leadership_strength} with specificity. Close with a direct offer for a brief conversation if they want feedback — not a vague "reach out anytime." Do not use corporate platitudes. This person is a peer.

**When to use it:** After an executive panel or board-level interview where the candidate gave significant time and you want to preserve the relationship.

**Pro tip:** Senior candidates talk. A poorly worded executive rejection can surface on LinkedIn, Glassdoor, or in a board room six months later. Review the output before sending and make sure {deciding_factor} reads as a business decision, not a personal judgement. "We prioritised a candidate with direct P&L accountability in regulated markets" is defensible. "Stronger cultural fit" is not.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who applied for a graduate or entry-level role and did not progress past the application stage.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Application volume for this role: {number_of_applications}
> Whether you reviewed their CV personally: {yes / no}
> Tone: {encouraging / neutral / brief}
> Company name: {company_name}
>
> Write a 100 to 150 word rejection email. Acknowledge the volume of applications briefly if relevant. Be clear they have not been progressed. If {yes}, mention you reviewed their application — it reads as respectful. If the tone is {encouraging}, close with one sentence of genuine encouragement tied to early-career progression — not generic "you have a bright future." Do not invite them to reapply unless there is a specific reason to.

**When to use it:** Graduate intake cycles where you have 300-plus applications and owe every applicant a human reply, not an auto-generated bounce.

**Pro tip:** For entry-level roles, candidates often screenshot rejection emails and share them — positively or negatively. If the tone is {encouraging}, the output tends to land well publicly. If you're rejecting 200 people with the same email, adjust {candidate_name} and keep everything else stable. ChatGPT can batch this if you give it a list.

## Rejection After Assessment or Skills Test

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who completed a take-home skills assessment or technical test but did not meet the required standard.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Assessment type: {assessment_type}
> Where the submission fell short (internal, paraphrase diplomatically): {assessment_gap}
> Time the candidate invested in the assessment: {estimated_hours}
> Tone: {respectful and direct / warm / formal}
>
> Write a 150 to 180 word rejection email. Open by acknowledging the time they put into the {assessment_type}. Be clear they are not progressing. Name the {assessment_gap} in one honest but diplomatic sentence — don't be vague. Close with thanks and respect for the effort. Do not suggest they "nearly made it" unless that is true.

**When to use it:** When a candidate submitted a coding test, writing sample, or case study and the result doesn't meet the bar, and you want to give them more than a form rejection.

**Pro tip:** Naming the {assessment_gap} specifically — even diplomatically — is more useful to the candidate than silence and dramatically reduces reply-for-feedback emails. "Your technical test showed strong logic but the solution did not scale beyond 10,000 records, which is a core requirement for this role" closes the loop better than "we have decided not to proceed."

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who passed the skills assessment but failed a required compliance check or background verification.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Check type that caused the issue: {check_type}
> Whether you can legally specify the reason: {yes / no — legal advice applied}
> HR contact for questions: {hr_contact_name}, {hr_contact_email}
> Tone: {neutral / formal}
>
> Write a 120 to 150 word rejection email. If {yes}, name the {check_type} as the reason clearly. If {no}, state the offer cannot be progressed at this time without elaborating — do not fabricate an alternative reason. In either case, provide the {hr_contact_name} and {hr_contact_email} as the point of contact for any queries. Do not apologise excessively. Keep the language legally neutral.

**When to use it:** After a conditional offer where a background check, right-to-work verification, or reference check has returned a result that prevents hiring.

**Pro tip:** If {yes} is "no" — meaning you cannot specify — do not let ChatGPT fill in the gap with a softened explanation. Review the output carefully and remove anything that implies a specific reason if legal has advised silence. The phrase "we are unable to proceed with your application at this time" is safe. "Unfortunately your background check flagged a concern" is only safe if your legal team has signed off on that level of disclosure.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who submitted a portfolio or work sample for a creative role that did not meet the brief.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title — e.g., Senior Copywriter, UX Designer}
> Portfolio issue (internal): {portfolio_gap}
> One element of their work you genuinely respected: {positive_observation}
> Whether you want to keep their details for freelance or future briefs: {yes / no}
> Tone: {constructive / warm / neutral}
>
> Write a 160 to 200 word rejection email. Acknowledge the submission specifically — not just "we reviewed your portfolio." Name the {positive_observation} in one genuine sentence. Explain the {portfolio_gap} in one honest sentence framed around fit for this brief, not talent. If {yes}, make the freelance or future offer concrete: one sentence explaining what type of work or when. Do not overpraise to soften the rejection.

**When to use it:** Rejecting a designer or creative professional after a portfolio review for a permanent role where the work showed ability but not the right specialisation.

**Pro tip:** Overpraise backfires badly in creative rejections — if you say "your work is exceptional" and then reject them, they will push back. The {portfolio_gap} should be framed as a mismatch ("the brief required B2B SaaS product experience and your portfolio focuses on consumer brand work") rather than a quality judgement. ChatGPT handles this framing well if your variable is specific.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who completed a full-day in-person assessment centre and was not selected.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role or programme: {job_title_or_programme}
> Assessment centre date: {assessment_date}
> Key competency they showed strength in: {strong_competency}
> Key competency where they did not reach the required level: {gap_competency}
> Whether written feedback report will follow separately: {yes / no}
> Tone: {direct / warm / formal}
>
> Write a 200 to 230 word rejection email. Open by acknowledging the full day they invested on {assessment_date}. Deliver the decision in the first paragraph — do not bury it at the end. Name the {strong_competency} in one sentence. Name the {gap_competency} in one sentence framed as the bar for this role, not a personal failing. If {yes}, confirm the feedback report is coming and give a rough timeframe. Close with respect and without hollow encouragement.

**When to use it:** Assessment centre results day — typically the morning after, when you need to notify 8 to 12 candidates who each gave an entire working day to the process.

**Pro tip:** Deliver the decision in the first paragraph. Candidates who have spent a full day at an assessment centre will read this email the moment it arrives — often on their phone, standing on a train platform. Burying the decision after two paragraphs of preamble is unkind. The output of this prompt opens with the decision by design; resist editing it to the bottom.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who completed an AI-proctored online assessment but whose results did not meet the benchmark for the next stage.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Assessment platform used: {assessment_platform}
> Whether results are shareable with the candidate: {yes / no}
> Benchmark score or descriptor (if shareable): {benchmark_detail}
> Tone: {neutral / direct}
>
> Write a 130 to 160 word rejection email. Be clear they are not progressing past the assessment stage. If {yes}, share the {benchmark_detail} plainly — no inflation or softening. If {no}, do not hint at the score. Include a sentence about the {assessment_platform} so they know which assessment this refers to. Do not imply the platform made the decision — the hiring team made the decision using the results. Close professionally without excessive warmth.

**When to use it:** High-volume graduate or early-career hiring where 500-plus candidates sit an online assessment and a significant proportion do not reach the cut score.

**Pro tip:** "The platform made the decision" language creates legal and reputational risk. Review ChatGPT's output for phrases like "our assessment tool has determined" and replace with "based on the results of your {assessment_platform} assessment, we have decided not to progress your application." The decision is always yours. The tool is evidence, not the decision-maker.

## Rejection for Internal Candidates

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to an internal employee who applied for a promotion or lateral move and was not selected.
>
> Employee name: {employee_name}
> Current role: {current_job_title}
> Role they applied for: {target_job_title}
> Whether they interviewed: {yes / no}
> Main reason not selected (internal framing): {rejection_reason}
> Whether their manager knows the outcome already: {yes / no}
> Tone: {direct and supportive / formal}
>
> Write a 180 to 220 word rejection email. Acknowledge the courage it took to apply internally. Deliver the decision clearly in the first or second paragraph. Name the {rejection_reason} in one honest, constructive sentence. If {yes — manager knows}, acknowledge that a follow-up conversation with their manager is already planned or expected. Close with a concrete next step — a development conversation, a specific skill to build — not a vague "let's talk soon."

**When to use it:** After an internal promotion round closes, before the successful candidate's announcement goes out — timing matters here.

**Pro tip:** Send this before the internal announcement, not after. An employee who finds out they didn't get the role through a company-wide congratulations email to someone else will not forget it. The {whether their manager knows} variable exists for this reason — coordinate with the line manager the same morning.
