## Post-Application Rejections (Volume Screening Stage)

> You are an HR manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who applied online and did not progress past the initial screening stage.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Department: {department}
> Reason for rejection (internal, not for sharing verbatim): {screening_reason — e.g. missing required certification, salary expectation mismatch, location constraint}
> Company tone: {warm_and_human / professional_and_brief / formal}
>
> Write a 120 to 160 word rejection email. Open by thanking the candidate for applying. Do not state the specific reason for rejection. Acknowledge the effort it takes to apply. Close with a genuine one-sentence encouragement to watch the company's careers page for future roles. Use plain English. No corporate filler phrases.

**When to use it:** Monday morning after a job posting closes and you have 40+ applicants to screen out before first interviews begin.

**Pro tip:** If your ATS populates first names automatically, remove the {candidate_name} variable and let your system handle personalisation. Manually adding "Dear [misspelled name]" defeats the purpose of using AI at all.

> You are an HR manager writing a bulk rejection email for candidates who applied for a role that has been put on hold or cancelled internally.
>
> Role title: {job_title}
> Reason for hold (internal): {internal_reason — e.g. headcount freeze, restructure, role redefined}
> How long they waited since applying: {weeks_since_application}
> Preferred tone: {apologetic_and_warm / neutral_and_professional}
> Recruiter name and contact: {recruiter_name}, {recruiter_email}
>
> Write a 130 to 170 word email explaining the role is no longer moving forward at this time. Do not disclose the internal reason. Acknowledge the wait time directly. Invite them to apply for future roles and offer {recruiter_name}'s email if they want to be considered for similar upcoming positions. No false promises about timelines.

**When to use it:** When leadership kills a req mid-pipeline and you're sitting on 15 applicants who deserve a straight answer.

**Pro tip:** Mentioning the wait time ("we know you applied six weeks ago") converts a generic email into something that actually acknowledges the candidate's experience. Skip it and the email reads like an automated dismiss.

> You are an HR manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who did not pass the resume screening stage because they were overqualified for the role.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Candidate's approximate seniority based on CV: {seniority_level — e.g. 15 years' experience, previous VP-level roles}
> Company size and stage: {company_size_and_stage — e.g. 80-person Series B startup}
> Tone: {honest_and_respectful / warm_and_brief}
>
> Write a 150 to 200 word rejection email. Be honest that the role is likely a step back from where they currently operate. Do not be condescending. Acknowledge their profile is strong. If the company has senior roles that open frequently, mention that in one sentence. Close with a direct, non-patronising sign-off.

**When to use it:** When a candidate with a C-suite background applies for a coordinator role and you want to treat them with the respect their experience deserves rather than sending a generic "we've moved forward with other candidates" line.

**Pro tip:** Do not use the phrase "you're overqualified" in the email itself — it reads dismissive. The prompt above handles this with honest framing without using the word.

> You are an HR manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who submitted a complete application but whose skills did not match the core requirements listed in the job description.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Key requirement they did not meet: {missing_requirement — e.g. no experience with Salesforce CRM, no degree in a regulated field, no hands-on management experience}
> Whether you want to encourage reapplication if they gain the skill: {yes / no}
> Tone: {direct / warm}
>
> Write a 140 to 180 word rejection email. Do not list every gap. Reference one core requirement in a neutral, non-judgmental way. If {yes}, close with a specific sentence inviting them to reapply once they have gained that experience. If {no}, close with a general wish of success in their search.

**When to use it:** When a candidate clearly put effort into their application but is missing a hard, non-negotiable requirement. The specific callout prevents them from applying again immediately for the same gap.

**Pro tip:** If your legal or compliance team has flagged candidate communications in the past, have this one reviewed before you use it at scale. Mentioning a specific missing skill — even neutrally — can occasionally trigger a complaint from a candidate who disputes the assessment.

> You are an HR manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who applied through an employee referral but did not meet the minimum requirements for the role.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Referring employee name: {referring_employee_name}
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Core gap: {core_gap — e.g. no relevant industry experience, missing required qualification}
> Tone: {warm_and_diplomatic / professional}
>
> Write a 150 to 190 word rejection email. Acknowledge the referral without naming the employee unless {candidate_name} already knows who referred them. Be clear the decision is about the fit for this specific role. Avoid any language that implies the referring employee made a mistake. Close warmly. This email may be forwarded to the referrer internally.

**When to use it:** When you need to reject a referred candidate and you're aware the email could easily land in the inbox of the colleague who made the referral.

**Pro tip:** Write this one as if the referring employee will read it. They often do. A cold rejection reflecting badly on their referral damages internal relationships and discourages future referrals.

## Post-Phone Screen or First Interview Rejections

> You are an HR manager writing a rejection email after a first-round phone screen where the candidate did not progress.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Date of phone screen: {screen_date}
> Primary reason for not progressing (internal only): {internal_reason — e.g. communication style not aligned, salary gap too large, motivation unclear}
> Whether to offer brief feedback: {yes_brief / no}
> Tone: {warm / professional}
>
> Write a 160 to 210 word rejection email. Thank them for their time on the call. Do not state the internal reason verbatim. If {yes_brief}, include one honest, constructive sentence of feedback that is specific enough to be useful but not detailed enough to invite a dispute. If {no}, keep the closing neutral. Do not use "we went with a stronger candidate" language.

**When to use it:** The day after a batch of phone screens when you need to close out 8 to 12 candidates before moving to panel interviews.

**Pro tip:** "One sentence of feedback" is deliberate. Two or more sentences invites the candidate to rebut each point by reply. One specific sentence — "we were looking for more demonstrated experience leading cross-functional projects" — is actionable without opening a back-and-forth.

> You are an HR manager writing a rejection email after a first in-person or video interview where the candidate showed potential but the team decided not to progress at this stage.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Interview date: {interview_date}
> What impressed the team: {one_genuine_positive — e.g. strong technical knowledge, excellent cultural alignment, clear communication style}
> Primary gap: {primary_gap — e.g. not enough leadership experience for this level, narrower domain expertise than needed}
> Whether to keep in talent pool: {yes / no}
> Tone: {warm_and_genuine / professional_and_brief}
>
> Write a 180 to 230 word rejection email. Open with a specific, genuine compliment tied to {one_genuine_positive}. Be clear the decision is about this role at this time. Address the gap in one sentence without dwelling on it. If {yes}, explicitly ask their permission to keep their details and reach out for future roles. If {no}, close cleanly.

**When to use it:** When a candidate genuinely impressed the panel but lost out to someone further along in their career. This email protects the employer brand and keeps a warm candidate available.

**Pro tip:** The permission-to-retain ask ("may we keep your details on file?") matters legally in jurisdictions with GDPR or similar data rules. If you're operating in the EU or UK, make this yes/no explicit and log their response.

> You are an HR manager writing a rejection email after a first interview where the candidate was visibly a poor cultural fit but technically qualified on paper.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Team structure they'd join: {team_description — e.g. fully remote team of 6, highly collaborative in-person environment, fast-moving startup team}
> Nature of the fit concern (internal, do not state directly): {fit_concern — e.g. very siloed working style, overly hierarchical expectations, low adaptability to ambiguity}
> Tone: {diplomatic / warm_and_honest}
>
> Write a 160 to 200 word rejection email. Acknowledge their qualifications without over-praising. Use neutral "working style and team fit" language — do not name specific personality traits or behaviours. Do not imply they did anything wrong. Close respectfully. Keep every sentence legally defensible.

**When to use it:** When the interview debrief was unanimous — "great CV, wrong fit" — and you need to draft the email without putting anything in writing that could be misread as discriminatory.

**Pro tip:** "Working style and team fit" is the safest framing for cultural rejection. Avoid phrases like "personality fit", "energy", "vibes", or "attitude" — all of these have appeared in employment tribunal cases. Review your company's recruitment policy before adding any culture-based language.

> You are an HR manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who interviewed well but where the salary expectation was materially above the approved budget for the role.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Approved salary band for role: {approved_band — e.g. £42,000 to £48,000}
> Candidate's stated expectation: {candidate_expectation — e.g. £58,000}
> Whether the band may widen in future: {yes / no / unclear}
> Tone: {direct_and_respectful / warm}
>
> Write a 150 to 190 word rejection email. Be honest that the role's budget and their expectations are not aligned. Do not apologise excessively. Do not imply they are worth less than they're asking. If {yes}, note the possibility of future roles at a higher grade. Do not disclose internal salary band figures unless your policy allows it.

**When to use it:** When a strong candidate told HR on the screen their number was firm and it's 25% above the approved band. Sending a vague "went with another candidate" email when both parties know the real reason is a waste of everyone's trust.

**Pro tip:** If your company publicly posts salary ranges (increasingly required in many US states and the EU as of 2026), remove the variable about not disclosing the band and instead reference the posted range directly — it removes ambiguity and shows good faith.

> You are an HR manager writing a short rejection email after a first video interview for a fully remote role where the candidate's availability or timezone did not align with the team's core hours.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Team's core hours and timezone: {team_timezone — e.g. 9am to 3pm CET overlap required}
> Candidate's timezone or availability issue: {candidate_availability — e.g. based in GMT-8, unavailable before 2pm CET}
> Tone: {neutral_and_brief / warm}
>
> Write a 120 to 150 word rejection email. Be specific that this is a logistics and operational requirement, not a reflection of their suitability. State clearly what the team requires. Do not dress it up as something else. Close professionally. Leave the door open if the situation changes in future.

**When to use it:** When you're managing a distributed team with hard core-hours requirements and a candidate in a conflicting timezone applied without checking the requirement listed in the job ad.

**Pro tip:** Stating the specific overlap requirement in the email ("our team requires 9am to 3pm CET availability") gives the candidate something concrete. It also prevents them reapplying immediately for the same role without resolving the underlying issue.

## Final-Stage and Near-Miss Candidate Rejections

> You are an HR manager writing a rejection email to a finalist candidate — someone who reached the final round but was not selected. This person invested significant time in the process.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Number of interview rounds they completed: {rounds_completed — e.g. 3 rounds including a technical task}
> What they did well (genuinely): {genuine_strengths — two or three specific things}
> Why the other candidate was selected (internal): {decision_reason — e.g. the other candidate had direct category experience, deeper technical specialisation}
> Whether to offer a call: {yes — recruiter will call first / no}
> Tone: {warm_and_genuine / formal_and_respectful}
>
> Write a 250 to 320 word rejection email. Acknowledge the time and effort invested across {rounds_completed}. Name the specific strengths from {genuine_strengths} — do not be vague. Explain the final decision was between two strong candidates without fabricating detail. If {yes}, say the hiring manager or recruiter will call before this email to discuss. Do not use "we went in a different direction" or "not the right fit at this time" without supporting detail.

**When to use it:** After the final debrief when you've made an offer to your first-choice candidate and need to notify the runner-up before they call you asking for an update.

**Pro tip:** Call before you email for finalist candidates whenever possible. The email then serves as a follow-up record of the conversation rather than cold rejection after weeks of effort. If your team set the variable {yes}, make the call happen before the email sends — same day, ideally within the hour.
