## Post-Interview Rejection Emails

> You are an HR manager writing a candidate rejection email after a final-round interview.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Interview stage reached: {interview_stage — e.g. final round, second round}
> One genuine strength you observed: {observed_strength}
> Primary reason for not progressing (internal, do not include verbatim): {internal_reason}
> Tone: {tone — warm / professional / brief}
> Company name: {company_name}
>
> Write a 150 to 200 word rejection email. Open by thanking {candidate_name} by name for their time at the {interview_stage} stage. Acknowledge one genuine strength without overpromising a future role. Decline clearly in one sentence — no passive language. Close with a specific, sincere wish for their search. Do not mention the internal reason. Do not invite them to reapply unless the role is genuinely likely to reopen.

**When to use it:** Friday afternoon after a final-round debrief when you need to notify the silver-medallist candidate before the weekend.

**Pro tip:** If your internal reason is legally sensitive — such as the candidate being overqualified by salary expectation or the role being restructured — keep the {internal_reason} field vague in your prompt. The AI will still write a clean email, and you won't accidentally generate a paper trail that mirrors protected-class reasoning.

> You are an HR manager sending a post-first-interview rejection to a candidate who was technically qualified but not a culture or team fit.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Interview format: {interview_format — e.g. panel, one-to-one, group}
> Team dynamic that influenced the decision: {team_dynamic — e.g. highly autonomous environment, fast-paced delivery culture}
> Tone: {tone — direct / empathetic}
> Hiring manager sign-off name: {hiring_manager_name}
>
> Write a 120 to 160 word rejection email. Do not cite culture fit as a reason — this opens legal risk. Instead, frame the decision around the specific working style or environment described in {team_dynamic} without implying the candidate is deficient. Use one concrete sentence to close the door. Sign off from {hiring_manager_name} or the HR team as appropriate.

**When to use it:** When the panel agrees the candidate is capable but the fit decision is genuinely about working style, and you need to communicate this without creating a discrimination exposure.

**Pro tip:** Never let the AI use the phrase "we felt you weren't the right fit" or "culture fit" verbatim — both are vague enough to invite legal challenge. If you see either phrase in the output, regenerate with the instruction "avoid any reference to culture fit or personality".

> You are an HR manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who completed a technical assessment or take-home task but did not progress to interview.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Task or assessment completed: {task_description — e.g. 30-minute SQL test, take-home case study}
> Threshold not met (internal only): {threshold_detail}
> Whether feedback is being offered: {feedback_offered — yes / no}
> Tone: {tone — respectful / concise}
>
> Write a 130 to 170 word email. Acknowledge the time the candidate invested in completing {task_description}. Decline clearly without referencing the specific score or threshold. If {feedback_offered} is yes, include one sentence offering a brief written summary — do not promise a call unless your team has capacity. If {feedback_offered} is no, close without raising expectations of further contact.

**When to use it:** After an automated test round closes and you're batch-notifying the candidates who didn't clear the threshold before the next interview wave starts.

**Pro tip:** If you're sending this email to more than ten candidates from the same test round, vary the {task_description} phrasing slightly in each prompt run so the emails don't read identically if candidates compare notes — which they do, particularly in tight professional communities.

> You are an HR manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who was referred internally by an employee, and who interviewed but was not selected.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Internal referrer name: {referrer_name}
> Stage reached: {interview_stage}
> Genuine positive from the process: {genuine_positive}
> Whether the referrer has already been told: {referrer_notified — yes / no}
> Tone: {tone — warm / professional}
>
> Write a 160 to 200 word rejection email addressed to {candidate_name}. Acknowledge their connection to the company through {referrer_name} without making that connection feel transactional. Decline clearly. Include {genuine_positive} as a specific observation, not a generic compliment. Do not mention that {referrer_name} was or was not notified — leave that relationship to handle separately.

**When to use it:** When an employee refers a friend or family member who interviews well but doesn't land the role, and you need to protect both relationships simultaneously.

**Pro tip:** Send this email before the referrer finds out via the candidate — if the referred person hears the news first and feels the email was cold or generic, the referrer's trust in your process takes the hit. Use the {genuine_positive} field to write something specific enough that it clearly came from the actual interview.

> You are an HR manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who applied for a senior leadership role and reached the final stage but lost to a stronger candidate on one specific dimension.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title — e.g. Head of Operations, VP of Sales}
> Deciding factor (internal, do not include verbatim): {deciding_factor — e.g. P&L ownership depth, board-level communication experience}
> Time spent in process: {weeks_in_process}
> Whether you want to keep them warm for future roles: {keep_warm — yes / no}
> Tone: {tone — senior / respectful / direct}
>
> Write a 200 to 250 word rejection email. Senior candidates have invested significant time — acknowledge {weeks_in_process} explicitly. Decline without softening to the point of ambiguity. If {keep_warm} is yes, include a specific commitment such as a follow-up call in Q4 or a named future search — do not use vague language like "we'll keep you in mind". If {keep_warm} is no, close cleanly.

**When to use it:** After a final board-level debrief where the decision was genuinely close and the losing candidate is credible enough to resurface in your network within six months.

**Pro tip:** Senior candidates will read this email more carefully than junior ones. If the AI outputs anything that feels like a consolation prize — phrases like "you should be proud" or "this was an incredibly competitive process" — cut them. These candidates know what they are and condescension damages your employer brand at exactly the seniority level you can least afford it.

## High-Volume and Early-Stage Rejection Emails

> You are an HR manager writing a rejection email to candidates who applied but were screened out at the CV review stage before any interview.
>
> Role: {job_title}
> Volume of rejections this batch: {batch_size — e.g. 45 candidates}
> Core reason candidates did not progress (general, not candidate-specific): {screen_out_reason — e.g. did not meet minimum years of experience, role required UK right to work}
> Whether applications are still open: {applications_open — yes / no}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Tone: {tone — professional / brief}
>
> Write a 90 to 120 word rejection email suitable for sending to all {batch_size} candidates at this stage. Do not reference individual CV details. Decline clearly. If {applications_open} is yes, you may mention related open roles on the careers page — do not promise a specific role. Keep the language respectful but not effusive. This email will be sent at volume — it must not read as a personal letter.

**When to use it:** Monday morning after a weekend application window closes and you need to clear a backlog before shortlisting begins.

**Pro tip:** Ask ChatGPT to write two versions — one for candidates who met the minimum experience threshold but lacked something else, and one for those who didn't meet the minimum at all. The tone should be slightly warmer in the first case. Sending the same text to both groups when one group came close will cost you future applications.

> You are an HR manager writing a rejection email for candidates who applied via a job board for a role that has now been filled internally or cancelled before external interviews began.
>
> Role: {job_title}
> Reason process ended: {process_end_reason — internal hire / role cancelled / headcount freeze}
> Time since application received: {weeks_since_application — e.g. 3 weeks}
> Whether the role may reopen: {role_may_reopen — yes / no / unknown}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Tone: {tone — honest / brief}
>
> Write a 100 to 130 word email. Be honest that the role is no longer open without revealing internal business reasons. Apologise briefly for the time elapsed since {weeks_since_application}. If {role_may_reopen} is yes, include a single sentence about subscribing to job alerts — do not make it sound like a priority. Do not invite candidates to reapply for a role that does not exist.

**When to use it:** When a headcount freeze hits mid-search and you have 60 applicants who applied in good faith and deserve a straight answer.

**Pro tip:** The phrase "the role has been filled" when it hasn't is false and will occasionally get called out by candidates who see the job reposted. Use "the role is no longer available externally" — accurate for both an internal hire and a cancelled position.

> You are an HR manager writing a rejection email to candidates who completed a phone screen but did not progress to a structured interview.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Screener name: {screener_name}
> Duration of phone screen: {screen_duration — e.g. 20 minutes}
> One thing that came through positively in the call: {positive_from_call}
> Tone: {tone — warm / professional}
>
> Write a 120 to 150 word rejection email. Reference the phone conversation with {screener_name} to personalise it. Include {positive_from_call} as a specific observation — one sentence only. Decline clearly without blaming a "strong field" — this reads as a hedge. Close without promising a future role unless your process genuinely allows it.

**When to use it:** When you've run a batch of 20-minute phone screens and need to send personalised-feeling rejections without individually drafting each one.

**Pro tip:** The variable {positive_from_call} is the one doing most of the emotional work here. The more specific it is — "your experience managing distributed teams came through clearly" rather than "your enthusiasm" — the more the candidate feels seen. Brief your recruiters to note one real thing per screen call so this variable has real content.

> You are an HR manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who applied for a graduate scheme or entry-level role and did not progress past the application stage.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Programme or role: {programme_name — e.g. 2026 Graduate Engineering Scheme}
> Application volume this cycle: {application_volume — e.g. 800 applications for 12 places}
> Whether they can reapply next cycle: {reapply_eligible — yes / no}
> Next cycle opening date if known: {next_cycle_date — e.g. September 2026 or "not yet confirmed"}
> Tone: {tone — encouraging / professional}
>
> Write a 130 to 160 word rejection email. Acknowledge the competitiveness of {programme_name} with a real number from {application_volume} if it adds context, not as a softener. Decline clearly. If {reapply_eligible} is yes, tell them exactly what to do — include the {next_cycle_date} if known. Do not patronise. Graduate candidates are often told "you have a bright future" by every rejecting company — do not add to that noise.

**When to use it:** At the end of a graduate application window when you're notifying the majority who didn't make the assessment centre shortlist.

**Pro tip:** For graduate schemes, candidates often share rejection emails in online forums. A rejection email that reads as genuine and specific will be quoted positively and build your employer brand among exactly the talent you want to attract in future cycles. A generic one does the opposite.

> You are an HR manager writing a batch rejection email to candidates who registered interest in a role during a talent pipeline or expression-of-interest campaign, before a formal vacancy opened.
>
> Role or talent pool: {talent_pool_name — e.g. Senior Data Analyst Pipeline}
> Reason for closing or not progressing: {closure_reason — e.g. role has been filled from existing pipeline, headcount not approved}
> Time candidates have been waiting: {wait_time — e.g. 4 months}
> Whether to invite them to a different open role: {cross_role_invite — yes / no, and if yes: {alternative_role_link}}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Tone: {tone — honest / respectful}
>
> Write a 110 to 140 word email. Acknowledge {wait_time} directly — candidates who waited months deserve that acknowledgement. Explain {closure_reason} in plain language without corporate softening. If {cross_role_invite} is yes, include a single clear sentence with {alternative_role_link}. Do not promise to keep their details on file unless your ATS actually allows that and someone will act on it.

**When to use it:** When a talent pool you opened speculatively doesn't convert to a live role and you need to close it out without destroying goodwill.

**Pro tip:** "We'll keep your CV on file" is the most broken promise in recruitment. If your ATS has an active pipeline function and someone will actually search it in future, say so specifically: "Your profile will remain in our {talent_pool_name} folder, reviewed when a vacancy opens." If it won't, say nothing. Candidates know the difference.
