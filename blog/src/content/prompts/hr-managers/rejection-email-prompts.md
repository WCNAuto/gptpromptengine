## Early-Stage Rejections: Post-Application, Pre-Interview

> You are an HR Manager sending a post-application rejection email to a candidate who applied but was not selected for an interview.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Department: {department}
> Reason for not advancing (internal only, do not quote verbatim): {internal_reason — e.g. missing required qualification, volume of stronger applicants, role requirement mismatch}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Tone: {warm and brief / professional and neutral / formal}
>
> Write a 120 to 150 word rejection email. Open by thanking them for applying. State clearly they have not been selected to move forward. Do not explain the internal reason or invite unnecessary follow-up questions. Close with a genuine but non-committal line encouraging them to apply to future roles. Subject line included.

**When to use it:** When you're clearing a backlog of applicants at the end of a posting window and need to send 30 rejections in one sitting without each one reading like a copy-paste form letter.

**Pro tip:** Vary the {tone} field across batches. "Warm and brief" works for roles where the candidate pool is local or the brand is consumer-facing. "Professional and neutral" protects you legally if a candidate later disputes the decision — it gives them nothing to argue against.

> You are an HR Manager sending a rejection email to a candidate who submitted an application but is clearly underqualified for the role — missing the minimum stated requirements.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Missing requirement: {specific_missing_requirement — e.g. degree in engineering, 5 years minimum experience, active professional licence}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Was there a related role they might suit? {yes — {related_role_title} / no}
> Tone: {direct / empathetic}
>
> Write a 130 to 160 word rejection email. Be direct that the application does not meet the minimum requirements for this role — do not soften it to the point of ambiguity. If a related role exists, mention it briefly in one sentence. Do not invite them to reapply for this role. Subject line included.

**When to use it:** When a candidate has applied despite not meeting the posted minimums — common in roles requiring licences, certifications, or regulated experience — and you need to close the loop without leaving the door open to the wrong role.

**Pro tip:** If {missing_requirement} is a professional licence or legal requirement (e.g. a nursing registration or security clearance), run the email past legal before sending. The AI output is a clean draft, but regulatory language in rejection emails can carry liability depending on jurisdiction.

> You are an HR Manager sending a rejection to a candidate who applied for a role that has since been paused, frozen, or cancelled by the business — not due to the candidate's suitability.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Reason for pause (do not use these exact words): {role_frozen_due_to — e.g. budget freeze, restructure, headcount reduction, role scope changed}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Likelihood of role returning: {likely within 6 months / unclear / unlikely}
> Tone: {transparent and warm / professional and brief}
>
> Write a 140 to 170 word rejection email. Explain clearly that the role will not be progressing at this time and the decision has nothing to do with the candidate. If the role may return, say so honestly without making a promise. Do not over-explain internal business decisions. Subject line included.

**When to use it:** During a headcount freeze or post-merger restructure when candidates are in the pipeline and you need to close them out without creating brand damage or triggering complaints.

**Pro tip:** Avoid language like "business reasons" with no elaboration — candidates read it as evasive. "The role has been paused due to a change in our hiring plan" is more honest and generates fewer follow-up calls than deliberately vague wording.

> You are an HR Manager sending a rejection email to a candidate who applied for a role already filled by an internal candidate before the external posting closed.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Did the candidate submit a strong application regardless? {yes / no}
> Are there open roles they might suit? {yes — briefly describe: {open_roles_summary} / no}
> Tone: {appreciative and honest / neutral and brief}
>
> Write a 130 to 160 word rejection email. Be honest that the role was filled internally — candidates respect transparency over vague wording. Thank them for their time. If they were a strong applicant and there are open roles, mention those naturally in one sentence. Subject line included.

**When to use it:** When your internal mobility programme moves faster than your ATS closes external postings — a common gap that leaves candidates waiting with no explanation.

**Pro tip:** If you have a talent pool or CRM, use this email to explicitly invite the candidate to join it. Add one sentence: "I'm adding your details to our talent pool with your permission." Consent collected, relationship maintained, future pipeline built.

> You are an HR Manager sending a high-volume batch rejection email to candidates who applied for a graduate scheme or entry-level programme and were not selected for the assessment stage.
>
> Programme name: {programme_name}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Number of applicants (approximate, for context, not stated in email): {applicant_volume}
> Stage they were rejected at: {application sift / online test / video screening}
> Encouragement to reapply next cycle? {yes / no}
> Tone: {warm and encouraging / neutral and professional}
>
> Write a 110 to 140 word rejection email suitable for sending to all candidates rejected at this stage. It must feel personal in tone despite being standardised. Do not mention the total applicant number. Acknowledge the effort of applying. If reapplication is encouraged, say so in the final line. Subject line included.

**When to use it:** After closing a graduate intake where you may have 400 to 2,000 applicants to notify at the sift stage and personalising each one is not feasible — but a robotic form letter will tank your employer brand.

**Pro tip:** For graduate schemes specifically, the rejected candidate's friend might be your ideal hire next year. Tone matters beyond the individual. Set {tone} to "warm and encouraging" even if the underlying business is formal — this audience responds to it and shares rejection experiences on forums.

## Phone Screen and First-Round Interview Rejections

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who completed a phone screen but will not be progressing to a first-round interview.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Phone screen conducted by: {screener_name}
> Primary reason for not advancing (internal — do not quote directly): {internal_reason — e.g. salary expectations out of range, insufficient experience in {skill_area}, communication style not suited to role}
> Specific feedback to share? {yes — {one_line_of_honest_feedback} / no — keep it general}
> Tone: {warm / direct / formal}
>
> Write a 150 to 180 word rejection email. Thank the candidate for their time on the call. State clearly they will not be progressing. If specific feedback is provided, include it in one concise, professional sentence framed as "one thing that may be helpful." Do not give a vague reason if a real one has been provided. Close with a brief positive note. Subject line included.

**When to use it:** Monday morning after a week of phone screens when you have six or more candidates to close out before the hiring manager asks for an update on who's moving forward.

**Pro tip:** The {specific_feedback} field is where most HR Managers under-deliver. Candidates who got on a call have invested 20 to 30 minutes. One honest sentence — "Your experience is strong but the salary range is likely below where you're operating" — saves them applying to three more roles at your company that will also disappoint them.

> You are an HR Manager sending a rejection email after a first-round panel interview to a candidate who performed adequately but was not the strongest in the cohort.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Interview panel included: {panel_members_and_titles — e.g. "Sarah Chen, Hiring Manager; James Okonkwo, Team Lead"}
> Overall impression of candidate: {positive but outcompeted / average performance / strong on technical, weak on culture fit}
> Feedback to share: {yes — {feedback_summary_in_one_or_two_sentences} / no}
> Tone: {warm and honest / professional and brief}
>
> Write a 170 to 200 word rejection email. Open by naming the interview panel to make it feel personal. Acknowledge the candidate's strengths before delivering the decision. If feedback is provided, frame it as developmental, not critical. Do not use phrases like "we had many strong candidates" — it reads as filler. Subject line included.

**When to use it:** After first-round interview debriefs when you have three or four candidates to decline and the hiring manager wants closure on the shortlist by end of week.

**Pro tip:** Strike the phrase "we had many strong candidates" from every prompt output before sending. It's true, but candidates hear it as "we don't want to tell you why." Replace it with even a vague qualitative point — "the panel felt your background was stronger in X than in Y, which was the core of this role" — and the email reads as genuine.

> You are an HR Manager sending a rejection email to a candidate who interviewed well but whose salary expectations significantly exceed the approved budget for the role.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Candidate's stated salary expectation: {candidate_salary_expectation}
> Approved salary band for role: {approved_salary_band}
> Is there flexibility on the band? {no / minor — up to {flexibility_amount}}
> Tone: {honest and respectful / brief and professional}
>
> Write a 150 to 170 word rejection email. Be direct that the role's compensation does not align with the candidate's expectations and proceeding would not be fair to either party. Do not apologise excessively. If there is minor flexibility, acknowledge it but do not oversell it. Leave the door open for roles at a higher band in future. Subject line included.

**When to use it:** When you've run a first-round interview and the debrief surfaces a salary mismatch that wasn't caught at screening — you need to exit the candidate cleanly without implying the role pays more than it does.

**Pro tip:** Never write the actual salary band into the email unless your company has a pay transparency policy. Reference the mismatch by describing it ("the range for this role is below where you're operating") rather than citing exact figures that could be forwarded or shared publicly.

> You are an HR Manager sending a rejection email to a candidate who completed a first-round interview but withdrew interest partway through the process, then re-engaged, and has now been formally declined.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Reason candidate is being declined: {decision_reason — e.g. role filled, candidate deprioritised after withdrawal, stronger shortlist formed during the gap}
> Tone required: {professional and clear / warm but firm}
> Any future interest in this candidate? {yes / no}
>
> Write a 140 to 160 word rejection email. Acknowledge the candidate re-engaged after a gap without referencing it negatively. State the decision clearly. If there is future interest, include one genuine sentence about keeping them in mind — if not, close cleanly. Do not over-explain the timeline. Subject line included.

**When to use it:** When a candidate went cold, you filled the shortlist, they came back, and now you need to formally close the loop without the email reading as punitive or passive-aggressive.

**Pro tip:** Avoid any language that implies the candidate's earlier withdrawal caused the rejection — even if it did. "We moved forward with other candidates during that period" is clean. "After your earlier withdrawal we had to continue" assigns blame and opens a grievance risk.

> You are an HR Manager sending a rejection email to a candidate who failed a required skills test or technical assessment before the first formal interview.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Assessment type: {assessment_name — e.g. coding test, Excel proficiency test, written case study, data analysis task}
> General reason for not passing (do not share scores unless policy requires): {general_reason — e.g. did not meet the required threshold, approach did not match the technical requirements of the role}
> Can they reapply after upskilling? {yes — after {reapply_timeframe} / no}
> Tone: {encouraging / neutral and professional}
>
> Write a 140 to 160 word rejection email. Acknowledge that they completed the assessment — that effort counts. State they did not progress without sharing exact scores unless specified. Do not be vague to the point of uselessness. If reapplication is possible after upskilling, mention it clearly. Subject line included.

**When to use it:** After a technical screen wave — common in engineering, finance, or data roles — when you have 10 to 20 candidates who sat the assessment and need to be notified before the shortlist is briefed to the hiring manager.

**Pro tip:** If your ATS or assessment platform sends automated pass/fail notifications, check whether the candidate has already received a system message before sending this email. Sending a warm human rejection after a cold automated fail — or sending two contradictory messages — undermines trust in the process.

## Final-Round and Post-Offer Rejections

> You are an HR Manager sending a rejection email to a candidate who reached the final round of interviews but was not selected — the offer went to another finalist.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Number of final-round candidates: {finalist_count}
> How long was the candidate in the process: {process_duration — e.g. 6 weeks, 3 interview stages}
> Specific strength to acknowledge: {genuine_strength — e.g. strategic thinking, stakeholder communication, technical depth}
> Is this a candidate worth keeping in the talent pool? {yes / no}
> Tone: {warm and substantive / formal and respectful}
>
> Write a 200 to 240 word rejection email. Open with genuine recognition of how far they progressed and how long the process took. Name the specific strength the hiring team noted. State clearly the decision went to another candidate — do not make it sound provisional. If they're talent pool material, close with a direct, specific ask to stay in touch. Subject line included.

**When to use it:** When the final debrief is done, the offer is verbal with the preferred candidate, and you have one or two finalists who invested 6 or more weeks and deserve more than a paragraph.

**Pro tip:** Do not send this email until the preferred candidate has verbally accepted — or at minimum, you're confident they will. Sending a final-round rejection and then having the preferred candidate decline puts you in an impossible position where you have to call the rejected finalist back. That call is far harder than the email.
