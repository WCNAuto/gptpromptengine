## Post-Application Rejections (Before Interview Stage)

> You are an HR Manager sending a post-application rejection email to a candidate who applied but will not be progressed to interview.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role they applied for: {job_title}
> Department: {department}
> Reason for not progressing (internal only, inform tone but do not state directly): {internal_reason}
> Company tone: {formal / warm / neutral}
> Encourage to reapply in future: {yes / no}
>
> Write a 120 to 150 word rejection email. Open by thanking the candidate for their application. Acknowledge the volume of applications without using it as an excuse. Close with a single sentence that either invites or does not invite future applications based on the variable above. Do not mention the internal reason. Do not use phrases like "we regret to inform you" or "unfortunately at this time".

**When to use it:** Monday morning when you have 40 post-weekend applications to clear and need to send rejections before the week fills up.

**Pro tip:** If {internal_reason} is "ATS keyword mismatch" or "missing required qualification", flip {encourage_to_reapply} to "yes" — these candidates may be a fit for a different role or future intake and keeping the door open costs nothing.

> You are an HR Manager sending a high-volume batch rejection after a job posting closed with more applications than anticipated.
>
> Role: {job_title}
> Closing date of posting: {closing_date}
> Number of applications received (approximate, for context only): {application_volume}
> One specific quality you genuinely valued in applicants at this stage: {valued_quality}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Tone: {warm / neutral / formal}
>
> Write a 100 to 130 word rejection email that can be sent to all applicants who did not make the shortlist. It must feel personal enough that it does not read like a mail-merge. Use {valued_quality} to add one genuine line of acknowledgment. Do not promise feedback. Do not use passive voice in more than one sentence.

**When to use it:** When a role has closed and you need to clear 200-plus rejections in a single send. Personalising the {valued_quality} field — even to something like "your sector-specific experience" — lifts reply rates and candidate NPS.

**Pro tip:** Avoid pasting the actual {application_volume} into the email. It can make smaller volumes feel underwhelming or larger volumes feel like an excuse. Keep that variable as context for the AI only.

> You are an HR Manager rejecting a candidate whose application arrived after the role was already filled internally.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Date they applied: {application_date}
> Reason role is no longer available: internal hire (do not state this explicitly)
> Company name: {company_name}
> Will a similar role open soon: {yes / no / unknown}
> Tone: {warm / neutral}
>
> Write a 110 to 140 word rejection email. Be honest that the role is no longer available without disclosing that it was filled internally. If {will_similar_role_open} is "yes", include one sentence inviting them to watch the careers page. Keep the tone {tone}. No hollow apologies.

**When to use it:** When an internal promotion or transfer happened mid-campaign and external applicants are still sitting in your inbox uncontacted.

**Pro tip:** "The role is no longer available" is legally cleaner than "the position has been filled" in most jurisdictions because it avoids implying a selection decision was made about the candidate. Ask your employment lawyer to confirm for your region, but this wording is the safer default.

> You are an HR Manager sending a rejection to a candidate who applied for a role that has been placed on hold or cancelled.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role title: {job_title}
> Reason for hold or cancellation (internal context only): {internal_reason}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Is the candidate welcome to apply for other open roles: {yes / no}
> Tone: {formal / neutral}
>
> Write a 120 to 150 word rejection email. Explain that the role is on hold or has been cancelled without giving the internal reason. If {open_to_other_roles} is "yes", include one brief sentence pointing them to the careers page. Avoid sounding apologetic to the point of vagueness. One direct sentence up front, then context, then close.

**When to use it:** After a headcount freeze or budget cut that kills open roles mid-process. Candidates left hanging are a brand risk — this email closes the loop cleanly.

**Pro tip:** If you anticipate the role coming back within 90 days, add a line asking if you can keep their details on file. This saves you a re-sourcing cycle and the candidate appreciates the transparency.

> You are an HR Manager sending a rejection to a candidate who is underqualified for a posted role but who you want to keep engaged for a junior opening.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role they applied for: {applied_role}
> Role you want to suggest instead: {suggested_role}
> Key gap that ruled them out for the original role: {qualification_gap}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Tone: {warm / neutral}
>
> Write a 150 to 180 word rejection email for {applied_role}. In the second paragraph, pivot naturally to mention {suggested_role} as a better-fit opportunity. Reference {qualification_gap} only once and frame it as a question of fit, not failure. Close with a clear call to action — either a link to apply or an invitation to reply. Do not oversell the pivot or make it sound like a consolation prize.

**When to use it:** When a strong candidate clearly misread the seniority level of a role and you have a live junior vacancy that suits them.

**Pro tip:** Only use this prompt when {suggested_role} is genuinely open and posted. Sending a rejection that mentions a role that does not exist yet damages trust faster than a plain rejection.

## Post-Phone Screen Rejections

> You are an HR Manager sending a rejection email after a phone screen where the candidate did not meet the minimum requirements for the role.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Screener name (yours or a colleague's): {screener_name}
> The specific requirement not met (internal context, do not quote directly): {unmet_requirement}
> Tone: {warm / formal / neutral}
> Will you offer written feedback: {yes / no}
>
> Write a 130 to 160 word post-phone-screen rejection email. Thank the candidate for their time. Acknowledge one positive from the conversation without fabricating detail. Decline clearly in one sentence. If {offer_feedback} is "yes", offer a brief written note on request. If "no", do not mention feedback at all. No vague language like "we have decided to move forward with other candidates" — be direct without being blunt.

**When to use it:** Same day as the screen or the morning after. The longer you wait post-screen, the more likely the candidate follows up and creates more work.

**Pro tip:** "Acknowledge one positive" only works if {screener_name} leaves a note in your ATS after every call. Build that into your screening workflow — even a five-word note like "good communication, weak on compliance" gives you enough to personalise this email without fabricating.

> You are an HR Manager rejecting a candidate after a phone screen where the salary expectation was significantly above the approved band.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Approved salary band (do not include in email): {salary_band}
> Candidate's stated expectation (do not include in email): {candidate_expectation}
> Tone: {warm / direct}
> Company name: {company_name}
>
> Write a 120 to 150 word rejection email. State clearly that the role's compensation is not aligned with the candidate's expectation. Do not disclose the band or the candidate's figure. Do not leave the door open unless the band is genuinely negotiable. If the band is fixed, say so in plain language. One sentence of genuine thanks for their time. No hollow encouragement.

**When to use it:** When a candidate screened well but named a number 30 percent or more above band and continuing the process would waste both sides' time.

**Pro tip:** If your ATS auto-screens on salary range and you never spoke with the candidate, use the post-application prompt instead. This prompt is specifically for situations where a human had the conversation — the tone difference matters to the candidate.

> You are an HR Manager sending a rejection after a phone screen where the candidate's availability or location does not match the role's requirements.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Specific conflict (internal context only): {availability_or_location_conflict}
> Whether the conflict could be resolved in future: {yes / no}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Tone: {neutral / warm}
>
> Write a 110 to 140 word rejection email. Be clear the role requires something the candidate cannot currently meet, without stating the internal detail. Avoid implying the candidate did anything wrong. If {conflict_resolvable_in_future} is "yes", include one sentence inviting them to check back when their situation changes. Keep sentences short. No passive voice.

**When to use it:** When a candidate is London-based but the role requires three days on-site in Manchester, or when they cannot start for six months and you need someone in four weeks.

**Pro tip:** Do not include specifics like "you mentioned you cannot relocate" — this can feel invasive in writing even if it was said out loud. The prompt's phrasing keeps it neutral and avoids candidates feeling their personal circumstances are being held against them in a documented email.

> You are an HR Manager sending a rejection email after a phone screen where the candidate's English language proficiency was identified as a barrier to a client-facing role.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Nature of communication requirement (internal context): {communication_requirement}
> Tone: {respectful / neutral}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Whether other non-client-facing roles exist that could suit them: {yes / no}
>
> Write a 130 to 150 word rejection email. Frame the decision around the specific communication demands of the role, not the candidate's general ability. Do not use the phrase "language barrier". If {other_roles_available} is "yes", mention this briefly without listing roles. Close warmly but do not over-apologise. Legally defensible language — tie the requirement to role-specific duties only.

**When to use it:** After a screen for a role where verbal client communication is a genuine occupational requirement — call centre, client advisory, senior account management.

**Pro tip:** Run this output past your employment counsel before sending if your organisation is in a jurisdiction with strict language-discrimination protections. The prompt frames the requirement as role-specific, which is the right legal footing, but your counsel should confirm the final wording.

> You are an HR Manager sending a rejection after a phone screen where the candidate was strong but a more experienced shortlist has already been confirmed and there is no point progressing them further.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> One genuine strength from the screen: {candidate_strength}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Tone: {warm / professional}
> Open roles coming up that may suit them: {yes / no}
>
> Write a 140 to 170 word rejection email. Open with a specific reference to {candidate_strength} — not generic praise. Be honest that the shortlist is already confirmed at a higher experience level. Do not say "we have decided to move forward with other candidates" — explain the situation plainly. If {future_roles_likely} is "yes", ask permission to keep their details and be specific that it is for future roles, not a holding pattern.

**When to use it:** When you screened a promising graduate or mid-level candidate after your shortlist was already locked at senior level — a situation that happens when job ads stay live too long.

**Pro tip:** "Ask permission to keep their details" is not just courteous — in GDPR-regulated regions it is a legal requirement to obtain explicit consent before retaining candidate data for future use. This prompt builds that consent request into the email naturally.

## Post-Interview Rejections

> You are an HR Manager sending a post-interview rejection to a candidate who interviewed but did not progress to the next round.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Interview round they completed: {interview_round}
> One specific strength demonstrated in interview: {interview_strength}
> Primary reason for not progressing (internal only, inform tone): {rejection_reason}
> Whether you are offering feedback: {yes_verbal / yes_written / no}
> Tone: {warm / professional / formal}
>
> Write a 180 to 220 word post-interview rejection email. Name {interview_strength} specifically in the opening paragraph. Decline clearly without excessive apology. If {feedback_offer} is "yes_verbal", offer a 15-minute call. If "yes_written", offer a short written summary on request. If "no", close without mentioning feedback. End with one genuine wish for their search. Do not use "we went with someone with more experience" as a catch-all — be specific to the variables given.

**When to use it:** Within 48 hours of completing the interview round. Beyond 72 hours, candidate experience scores drop sharply and you start getting follow-up calls.

**Pro tip:** If {rejection_reason} is "culture fit", do not let that phrase appear anywhere in the email — in writing it reads as code for discrimination, even when it is not. The prompt will absorb that reason as tone context and produce neutral language. If you want to give feedback on culture fit, do it verbally only.

> You are an HR Manager sending a final-round rejection to a candidate who reached the last stage but was not selected.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Number of interview rounds they completed: {rounds_completed}
> Time they invested (approximate): {time_invested}
> The deciding factor that separated the hired candidate (internal context only): {deciding_factor}
> Tone: {warm / respectful}
> Company name: {company_name}
>
> Write a 200 to 250 word final-round rejection email. Acknowledge the significant time and effort invested without being sycophantic. Decline clearly in the second paragraph. Do not hint at or explain {deciding_factor}. Offer a feedback call as standard — at this stage it is expected. Close by leaving the door genuinely open for future roles if that is realistic, or close cleanly if it is not. No hollow lines like "we were blown away by every candidate."

**When to use it:** After a second or third interview, especially where the candidate completed a presentation, case study, or task. These candidates invested real time and a thin email does lasting brand damage.

**Pro tip:** For final-round rejections, send within 24 hours of the decision being made. If the hired candidate has not yet accepted, HR instinct is to wait — but candidates left in limbo for four or five days after a final interview frequently post negative Glassdoor reviews. Send the rejection before the offer is accepted if your legal team allows it.
