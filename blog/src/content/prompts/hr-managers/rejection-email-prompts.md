## Post-Phone-Screen Rejections

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who completed a phone screen but will not be progressed to interview.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Department: {department}
> Reason for rejection (internal only, do not state directly): {reason: salary_mismatch / missing_core_skill / location / culture_read}
> Tone: {warm_and_brief / professional_and_neutral}
> Company name: {company_name}
>
> Write a 100 to 130 word rejection email. Thank them for their time. Do not state the rejection reason explicitly. Close with a genuine encouragement to apply for future roles if appropriate. No hollow phrases like "we were blown away by the talent." Subject line included.

**When to use it:** Monday morning after a Friday phone screen block, when you have eight rejections to send before the hiring manager chases you.

**Pro tip:** If the real reason is salary, never hint at it in this email. Candidates sometimes reply asking for the actual figure and it opens a negotiation you don't want. Keep the language at "not the right fit at this time."

> You are an HR Manager sending a post-phone-screen rejection to a candidate who was genuinely strong but lost out to a better-matched applicant at the same stage.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Competing strength the candidate showed: {one_genuine_strength}
> Likelihood of future openings: {high / low / unknown}
> Tone: {warm / professional}
> Company name: {company_name}
>
> Write a 120 to 150 word rejection email that feels personal, not automated. Reference the strength they showed without overpromising a callback. If future openings are high, add one sentence inviting them to watch the careers page. Subject line included.

**When to use it:** When a candidate was genuinely impressive but the role had an unexpectedly strong applicant pool and you want to keep them warm for the next intake.

**Pro tip:** Adding even one specific genuine detail (e.g., "your experience with multi-site coordination stood out") cuts reply-asking-why rates by roughly half. Generic warmth reads as copy-paste and candidates know it.

>
> Industry: {industry}
> Common gap across this batch: {common_gap: experience_level / technical_skill / location / availability}

> Deadline sensitivity: {urgent: yes / no}
>
> Write a 90 to 110 word rejection email designed for volume sending. It must not feel like a mass email — avoid "Dear Candidate." Use a warm but efficient opening. No fabricated personal details. Subject line included.

**When to use it:** After closing applications on a high-volume role where 40+ candidates were screened in a single week and legal requires all to receive written notification.

**Pro tip:** For GDPR-compliant markets, add a line about data retention at the bottom ("We'll keep your details on file for 6 months unless you request deletion"). Paste it in once at the end before sending — ChatGPT won't add this unless you ask.

> You are an HR Manager rejecting a candidate after a phone screen because the role has been put on hold internally. The candidate did nothing wrong.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Reason for hold (internal): {budget_freeze / headcount_review / org_restructure}
> Whether the role may reopen: {likely / unlikely / uncertain}
> Tone: {honest_and_respectful / neutral}
> Company name: {company_name}
>
> Write a 120 to 140 word email explaining the role is on hold without specifying the internal reason. Do not say "we chose another candidate." Be clear this is not a reflection of the candidate. If the role may reopen, offer to keep their details. Subject line included.

**When to use it:** Mid-hiring-freeze when finance pulls sign-off mid-process and you have screened candidates waiting for interview invites you can no longer send.

**Pro tip:** Do not say "we hope to reopen this soon" unless you genuinely believe it. Candidates hold onto that sentence and follow up repeatedly. "We'll be in touch if the situation changes" is less committal and just as courteous.

>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Department they were interested in: {department}
> Strength noted during the call: {one_observed_strength}

> Company name: {company_name}
>
> Write a 110 to 130 word email declining the speculative interest. Acknowledge the conversation specifically. Do not invent future opportunities that don't exist. If reapply = yes, tell them clearly how to do it. Subject line included.

**When to use it:** After a speculative call you took as a favour to a referral or a LinkedIn inbound, and you need to close it professionally without stringing the person along.

**Pro tip:** If the referral was internal, BCC the referring colleague on this email rather than forwarding it afterward. It saves the candidate-asks-the-employee-for-an-update loop.

## Post-First-Interview Rejections

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who completed a first-stage interview but will not proceed to the next round.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Interview format: {video / in-person / panel}
> One genuine positive from the interview: {positive_observation}
> High-level gap (do not state verbatim in email): {gap: technical_depth / leadership_experience / culture_alignment / presentation_skills}
> Tone: {warm / direct / formal}
> Company name: {company_name}
>
> Write a 150 to 180 word rejection email. Open with thanks and one genuine observation. Decline clearly in the second paragraph — do not bury the news. Offer brief, non-litigious feedback in one sentence. Close professionally. Subject line included.

**When to use it:** The day after first-round interviews when the hiring manager has confirmed the shortlist and you need to notify everyone who didn't make it before word spreads.

**Pro tip:** "We've decided to move forward with other candidates whose experience more closely matches the role requirements" is legally safer than naming a specific gap. Save specific feedback for candidates who explicitly request it in a reply — then you control the format.

> You are an HR Manager rejecting a candidate after a first interview for a senior role ({seniority_level}: Director / VP / Head of) where the candidate was clearly over-experienced for the position.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Seniority level of role: {seniority_level}
> Approximate seniority gap: {one_to_two_levels_above / significantly_above}
> Tone: {respectful / honest}
> Company name: {company_name}
>
> Write a 140 to 160 word rejection email that acknowledges their strong background without being patronising. Be honest that the role scope may not match their experience level. Do not say "overqualified" — reframe around role fit and growth trajectory. Subject line included.

**When to use it:** When a candidate with 20 years of experience applied for a mid-level role and you interviewed them as a courtesy, but the hiring manager confirmed the mismatch is too wide.

**Pro tip:** "Overqualified" triggers defensiveness and occasional pushback emails. "The scope of this role may not fully leverage your background" lands better and rarely generates a reply arguing the point.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate after a first interview where their technical assessment results were below the benchmark.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Technical area assessed: {technical_area}
> Result summary (internal): {below_threshold / did_not_meet_minimum_score}
> Feedback you are willing to share externally: {general_feedback_one_sentence}
> Tone: {professional / neutral}
> Company name: {company_name}
>
> Write a 130 to 160 word rejection email. Thank them for completing the assessment. Decline clearly. Share the one sentence of external feedback naturally — do not make it sound like a report. Do not reveal the score or internal benchmark. Subject line included.

**When to use it:** After a technical screening round for engineering, data, or finance roles where pass/fail benchmarks are set before interviews begin.

**Pro tip:** If your ATS auto-sends a rejection before you send this email, candidates receive two notifications and it looks disorganised. Check your ATS automation triggers before running this prompt.

> You are an HR Manager writing a post-first-interview rejection email to a candidate who will not progress because the role requirements changed after the interview was scheduled.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> How requirements changed: {new_requirement_added / scope_expanded / seniority_lifted}
> Whether you want to keep the candidate in consideration for the original scope if it re-emerges: {yes / no}
> Tone: {transparent / professional}
> Company name: {company_name}
>
> Write a 130 to 150 word email that is honest about the change without making the company look disorganised. Make clear the candidate is not at fault. If yes to keeping them in consideration, say so specifically. Subject line included.

**When to use it:** When a hiring manager adds a must-have requirement mid-process (common after a competitor move or a new technology decision) and the existing interview pool no longer fits.

**Pro tip:** If you plan to keep their details, include a specific ask: "Would you be open to us reaching out in the next 90 days?" A yes/no question gets more replies than an open-ended "we'll be in touch."

**Pro tip:** If it's been more than ten days since their interview, acknowledge the wait in the first line: "I appreciate your patience while we completed our review." Ignoring the gap feels dismissive and increases negative Glassdoor reviews.

## Final-Round and Offer-Stage Rejections

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a final-round candidate who came second in a competitive process.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Number of final-round candidates: {final_round_count}
> One specific strength they demonstrated: {specific_strength}
> Whether you want to keep them warm for future roles: {yes / no}
> Tone: {warm / respectful / honest}
> Company name: {company_name}
>
> Write a 180 to 220 word rejection email. This candidate invested significant time. Acknowledge that explicitly. Name the specific strength — do not be generic. Decline clearly and early. If keeping warm = yes, include a concrete next step (e.g., check careers page, expect contact within 6 months). Subject line included.

**When to use it:** After a three-stage process where the final decision was close and the runner-up is someone you'd genuinely consider for the next opening.

**Pro tip:** Call before you email at this stage if possible. Email is fine for early-stage declines, but a candidate who completed three rounds and a case study deserves a phone call first. Use this prompt to draft the follow-up email you send after the call.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who was sent a verbal offer but the offer was subsequently withdrawn before a written contract was issued.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Reason for withdrawal (internal): {headcount_freeze / role_eliminated / budget_cut}
> Whether a financial goodwill gesture is being made: {yes / no}
> Legal sign-off obtained: {yes / no}
> Tone: {formal / empathetic}
> Company name: {company_name}
>
> Write a 160 to 200 word email withdrawing the verbal offer. Do not state the internal reason explicitly — use "a significant change in business circumstances." Express genuine regret. If goodwill = yes, reference it without specifying amount (legal will handle the detail). This email assumes legal sign-off has been confirmed. Subject line included.

**When to use it:** When a hiring freeze or org change hits after verbal acceptance but before the written offer is signed.

**Pro tip:** Do not send this email without HR legal review first. In several jurisdictions a verbal offer can constitute a binding agreement. This prompt produces a draft for review, not a final send-ready document at this stage.

>
> Write a 190 to 230 word rejection email that acknowledges the specific effort of the presentation. Name the topic they covered. Include the one approved piece of feedback naturally — not as a bullet point, as part of a sentence. Do not pad with generic encouragement. Subject line included.

**When to use it:** After a final-round assessment centre or take-home case study where candidates spent significant unpaid time preparing.

**Pro tip:** Get the hiring manager to approve the feedback wording in writing before you send this email. If the candidate pushes back on the feedback, you need to show you're quoting an agreed position, not your personal opinion.
