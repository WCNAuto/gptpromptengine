## Post-Application Rejections (Before Interview)

> You are an HR manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who applied for an open role but will not be moving forward to interview.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role applied for: {job_title}
> Department: {department}
> Reason for rejection (internal, do not quote directly): {rejection_reason: e.g. under-qualified, overqualified, role filled internally, high volume of stronger applicants}
> Company tone: {formal / warm-professional / direct}
> Keep the door open for future roles: {yes / no}
>
> Write a 120 to 150 word rejection email. Open by thanking the candidate for their time. In one sentence, confirm they will not be progressing. Do not give the internal rejection reason verbatim. If keeping the door open, close with one sentence inviting them to apply for future roles. No hollow flattery. No "we were blown away by your application."

**When to use it:** End of screening week when you have 40-plus applicants to cut and need a batch of slightly varied rejections that don't all read identically.

**Pro tip:** Run this prompt five times with different {candidate_name} values but keep all other variables the same. ChatGPT will naturally vary the phrasing each time, so you avoid sending the exact same email to candidates who know each other.

> You are an HR manager rejecting a candidate who applied speculatively — they sent a CV without responding to a posted vacancy.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Area of interest they mentioned: {candidate_area_of_interest}
> Current hiring status in that area: {hiring_status: e.g. no open roles, pipeline only, hiring freeze}
> Whether you want to retain their CV: {yes / no}
> Tone: {warm-professional / formal}
>
> Write a 100 to 130 word email. Acknowledge the speculative approach positively without promising anything. State clearly that there is no matching opening right now. If retaining their CV, say so explicitly and give a realistic timeframe for when you might be in touch. No vague "we'll keep you on file" without a timeframe.

**When to use it:** After the weekly LinkedIn or careers-page inbox clear-out when speculative applications have piled up from the previous fortnight.

**Pro tip:** If your ATS does not have a speculative pipeline, tell the candidate in this email what they should do to stay visible — follow your LinkedIn page, re-apply in six months. It prevents them emailing the same inbox repeatedly to chase.

> You are an HR manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who passed the initial CV screen but was screened out after an automated skills assessment or online test.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Assessment type: {assessment_type: e.g. numerical reasoning, coding test, situational judgment}
> Whether you can share their score: {yes / no}
> Minimum passing score or threshold (if sharing): {pass_threshold}
> Tone: {direct / warm-professional}
>
> Write a 130 to 160 word rejection email. Acknowledge they completed the assessment. State they did not meet the threshold for progressing. If sharing score, include one sentence with the result and the benchmark. Do not apologise excessively. Close with one sentence they can act on — retake eligibility, reapplication window, or a suggestion to build the relevant skill.

**When to use it:** The morning after automated assessment results come in and you need to notify the failed cohort before they start emailing to chase.

**Pro tip:** If your legal team has flagged that sharing scores creates risk, set {whether_you_can_share_their_score} to "no" and the prompt will omit it cleanly. Do not try to soften the no-score policy with vague language — candidates find "we cannot share assessment data at this stage" more credible than evasive phrasing.

> You are an HR manager rejecting a candidate because the role they applied for was closed or put on hold before interviewing began.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Reason for closure (internal only): {internal_reason: e.g. headcount freeze, role restructured, hired internally}
> What to tell the candidate: {candidate_facing_reason: e.g. the role has been put on hold, the position has been filled, organisational priorities have shifted}
> Whether to suggest similar roles: {yes / no}
> Similar role title (if yes): {similar_role}
>
> Write a 110 to 140 word email. Explain the situation honestly using the candidate-facing reason only. Apologise once — at the start — for the disruption to their time. If suggesting a similar role, name it specifically. Do not use the phrase "exciting opportunity."

**When to use it:** When a hiring freeze or reorg announcement hits mid-recruitment cycle and you need to notify the applicant pool the same day before word spreads on LinkedIn.

**Pro tip:** If you are in a publicly traded company and the freeze relates to unreleased financial news, run this email past legal before sending. The candidate-facing reason must be accurate but cannot pre-disclose material non-public information.

> You are an HR manager sending a bulk rejection to candidates who applied for a high-volume entry-level role and did not make the shortlist. You are writing one version of the email that will be lightly personalised at scale.
>
> Role: {job_title}
> Number of applicants (approximate, for your reference only): {applicant_volume}
> Key reason most were screened out: {primary_screen_out_reason: e.g. lacked required qualification, location not eligible, below minimum experience level}
> Tone: {warm-professional / direct}
> Brand voice note: {one_sentence_describing_company_tone: e.g. "we are a no-jargon tech startup", "we are a formal financial services firm"}
>
> Write a 100 to 120 word rejection email that reads as human even when sent at scale. Open with the candidate's first name placeholder {first_name}. Do not use "we regret to inform you." Do not include anything that requires individual customisation beyond the first name. Close with a single genuine sentence reflecting the brand voice note.

**When to use it:** Saturdays or early Monday mornings before the week's interviews begin, when clearing the application queue from the previous week's job ad traffic.

**Pro tip:** Paste this output into your ATS email merge and test it with three dummy records first. The {first_name} placeholder in the AI output must match your ATS merge field syntax exactly — if your system uses [[FIRST_NAME]], do a find-and-replace before uploading.

## Post-First Interview Rejections

> You are an HR manager writing a rejection email to a candidate after a first-round interview. The candidate interviewed well but another candidate was a stronger match.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Interview format: {interview_format: e.g. 30-minute video call, panel interview, phone screen}
> One genuine strength observed: {candidate_strength}
> Primary reason they did not progress (internal — do not quote verbatim): {rejection_reason}
> Tone: {warm-professional / direct}
> Keep the door open: {yes / no}
>
> Write a 150 to 180 word rejection email. Name the specific strength you observed — one concrete sentence, not generic praise. State clearly they will not be progressing to the next round. Do not explain the internal rejection reason. If keeping the door open, close with a sentence about future roles. Avoid "we had many strong candidates" as the sole explanation.

**When to use it:** Within 24 hours of completing first-round interviews, before the shortlist moves to second-round scheduling. Prompt rejection after first interview is one of the highest-leverage moments for candidate experience.

**Pro tip:** The {candidate_strength} variable is the one most HR managers skip because it takes effort. Resist that. Candidates who receive specific, genuine feedback after a first interview are significantly more likely to apply again and recommend the company. Even "your structured approach to the stakeholder scenario stood out" is enough.

> You are an HR manager rejecting a candidate after a first-round interview where the candidate clearly misrepresented their experience on their CV.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Misrepresentation area (internal reference only): {misrepresentation_area}
> Whether to reference the discrepancy in the email: {yes / no}
> Tone: {formal / direct}
> Legal sign-off obtained: {yes / no / not required}
>
> Write a 100 to 130 word rejection email. If {reference_the_discrepancy} is "yes," include one neutral sentence noting that the information discussed did not align with the application materials. Do not use accusatory language. If "no," reject without explanation beyond not progressing. Do not invite reapplication. Keep tone professional and final.

**When to use it:** When you have confirmed in the debrief that a candidate exaggerated qualifications, employment dates, or seniority and you need to close the process cleanly without creating a dispute.

**Pro tip:** If the misrepresentation is significant — fabricated credentials, falsified employment — set {legal_sign-off_obtained} to track that you have run this past HR legal before sending. A standard rejection email is usually safe, but the neutral disclosure sentence needs legal review if your company may take further action.

> You are an HR manager writing a rejection email after a first interview where the candidate's salary expectations are significantly above the approved budget for the role.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Approved salary range (internal): {approved_range}
> Candidate's stated expectation: {candidate_expectation}
> Whether to mention salary mismatch explicitly: {yes / no}
> Tone: {direct / warm-professional}
>
> Write a 120 to 150 word rejection email. If mentioning salary mismatch, state it plainly as a structural mismatch — not a judgement on the candidate's worth. Do not suggest they should accept less. If not mentioning it, reject without explanation. Do not leave ambiguity about whether they are still in consideration. Close the loop clearly.

**When to use it:** Immediately after a first-round call where salary expectations surfaced and you know there is no path to bridging the gap — saves both parties time before scheduling additional rounds.

**Pro tip:** If your company has pay transparency obligations (increasingly common under state and national legislation in 2026), confirm whether you are required to share the range in writing before setting {whether_to_mention_salary_mismatch} to "no." Omitting the range when you are legally required to disclose it creates more risk than the awkward conversation.

> You are an HR manager rejecting a candidate after a first interview because the role requires on-site presence and the candidate is not willing or able to relocate or commute.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Location requirement: {location_requirement: e.g. 5 days on-site in Manchester, 3 days in-office in Austin TX}
> Candidate's stated location situation: {candidate_location}
> Whether remote or hybrid options exist: {yes / no}
> Tone: {warm-professional / formal}
>
> Write a 110 to 140 word rejection email. State the location requirement clearly as the reason for not progressing. Do not frame it as the candidate's failure. If remote or hybrid options exist for other roles, mention them in one sentence. Do not suggest the requirement might change unless you know it will.

**When to use it:** After a screening call where location came up and you did not want to reject on the spot. Send this the same day — waiting longer signals false hope.

**Pro tip:** Do not soften this rejection with "we will keep your application for future remote opportunities" unless you have remote roles actively hiring. Empty keep-in-touch language damages trust when the candidate applies again and receives another rejection for the same reason.

> You are an HR manager writing a post-first-interview rejection to a candidate who was referred internally by an employee, so extra care is needed in tone.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Internal referrer: {referrer_name} (this name does not appear in the email — for your context only)
> Genuine reason for not progressing (internal): {rejection_reason}
> Whether to acknowledge the referral in the email: {yes / no}
> Tone: {warm-professional}
>
> Write a 150 to 170 word rejection email. Maintain professionalism — do not over-explain or over-apologise because of the referral. If acknowledging the referral, include one sentence thanking them for their interest in the company's work (not gushing). State clearly they are not progressing. Do not give the internal rejection reason. Close warmly but without ambiguity.

**When to use it:** When a hiring manager's contact, an executive referral, or an employee-referral-scheme candidate does not make the cut and the rejection needs to land well for two audiences — the candidate and the referrer.

**Pro tip:** Before sending, message the internal referrer privately to let them know the outcome. The rejection email alone is not enough. The referrer should not find out when the candidate tells them — that creates internal friction that falls back on HR.

## Post-Final Interview Rejections

> You are an HR manager writing a rejection email to a finalist candidate who reached the final round but was not selected. This is a high-stakes rejection — the candidate invested significant time.
>
> Candidate name: {candidate_name}
> Role: {job_title}
> Number of interview rounds they completed: {rounds_completed}
> Time investment (approximate): {time_invested: e.g. three interviews over four weeks, a take-home project plus two panel sessions}
> One or two specific strengths observed across the process: {observed_strengths}
> Whether to offer a feedback call: {yes / no}
> Tone: {warm-professional / formal}
>
> Write a 200 to 250 word rejection email. Acknowledge the time and effort invested without being excessive. Name one or two specific strengths — concrete, not generic. State clearly that another candidate was selected. Do not explain why the other candidate was stronger. If offering a feedback call, include a clear instruction for how to request it. Close with a sentence that is respectful and final.

**When to use it:** Within 48 hours of the final hiring decision being confirmed — finalist candidates are acutely aware of silence and will assume the worst while waiting.

**Pro tip:** If you offered a take-home project or presentation as part of the process and the candidate was not selected, acknowledge that specific effort by name in {time_invested}. "We know the business case presentation took significant preparation" lands very differently than a generic acknowledgement. It is the difference between a candidate who posts a negative Glassdoor review and one who reapplies in 18 months.
