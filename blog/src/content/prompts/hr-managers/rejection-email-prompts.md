## Post-Application Rejections (Before Interview Stage)

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who applied for an open role but will not be progressed to interview.
>
> Role they applied for: {job_title}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Candidate first name: {candidate_first_name}
> Reason for rejection (internal, do not state verbatim): {rejection_reason: e.g. lacking required qualification / overqualified / role filled internally / volume hire — too many applicants}
> Tone: {warm_and_brief / neutral_and_professional / formal}
> Future pipeline: {yes — invite to stay in touch / no — do not encourage reapplication}
>
> Write a 100 to 130 word rejection email. Do not mention the rejection reason explicitly. Open by thanking them for applying. State clearly they will not be progressed. If future pipeline is yes, close with one sentence inviting them to watch for future openings. No hollow phrases like "we were blown away by the volume of applications."

**When to use it:** Monday morning after closing a high-volume role. You have 40 rejections to send and need a base email you can personalise slightly per batch.

**Pro tip:** If your ATS auto-sends rejection emails, use this to rewrite the default ATS copy instead—most ATS default emails read like legal disclaimers. Paste the ATS template into ChatGPT alongside this prompt and tell it to rewrite, not start from scratch.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email for a candidate who applied speculatively (no open role posted) and cannot be considered at this time.
>
> Candidate first name: {candidate_first_name}
> Role or field they expressed interest in: {interest_area}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Likelihood of relevant opening in next 6 months: {high / low / unknown}
> Tone: {encouraging / neutral}
> Hiring freeze or headcount constraint active: {yes / no}
>
> Write a 110 to 140 word email. Acknowledge the speculative outreach. Decline clearly. If likelihood of opening is high, invite them to reapply when a role is posted. If hiring freeze is yes, say positions are limited without using the words "hiring freeze." Never say "we'll keep your CV on file" unless you actually will.

**When to use it:** After a candidate emails your careers inbox directly or reaches out via LinkedIn asking about opportunities. These rejections often get ignored, which damages employer brand.

**Pro tip:** The phrase "we'll keep your CV on file" creates an implied obligation in several jurisdictions. If your company has no active talent pool practice, this prompt's constraint stops ChatGPT from writing it in by default.

> You are an HR Manager writing a bulk rejection email for candidates who applied to a role that has now been put on hold or cancelled.
>
> Job title: {job_title}
> Company name: {company_name}
> Reason role is on hold: {internal — do not include in email: e.g. budget cut / reorg / role redesigned / filled by transfer}
> Number of candidates receiving this email: {approximate_number}
> Will the role reopen: {yes — likely within 3 months / no / unknown}
> Tone: {straightforward / apologetic / neutral}
>
> Write a 120 to 150 word email. Explain the role is no longer progressing without stating the internal reason. If the role will reopen, mention that without making a commitment. Thank them for their time. Do not use passive constructions like "a decision has been made." Write in first person from the HR Manager.

**When to use it:** When a headcount is pulled mid-process—often after candidates have already submitted assessments or taken time off for interviews. Getting this email out fast limits damage to candidate experience scores.

**Pro tip:** For this scenario, add one sentence at the end offering to connect candidates with your recruiter on LinkedIn. It costs nothing and consistently improves Glassdoor feedback on the rejection experience.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who was screened by phone but will not be progressed to a hiring manager interview.
>
> Candidate first name: {candidate_first_name}
> Job title: {job_title}
> Recruiter or HR Manager name sending the email: {sender_name}
> Primary reason not progressing (internal): {salary_expectations_misaligned / availability_doesnt_fit / skills_gap_identified / stronger_shortlist}
> Did you discuss next steps on the call: {yes — you indicated they would hear back / no}
> Tone: {warm / professional / brief}
>
> Write a 130 to 160 word rejection email. If you indicated they would hear back on the call, open by acknowledging that. Be clear they are not progressing. Do not fabricate specific feedback if no feedback variable is provided. Close with a genuine wish for their search. Sign off from {sender_name}.

**When to use it:** After phone screen week when you need to close out candidates who weren't shortlisted. Sending these within 48 hours of the call is the benchmark most candidates expect.

**Pro tip:** If the salary misalignment was significant, consider adding one sentence acknowledging it was a mutual fit issue rather than a one-sided rejection—candidates respond better and are less likely to leave negative reviews when they feel the outcome was logical.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who completed a take-home assessment or skills test but will not be progressed further.
>
> Candidate first name: {candidate_first_name}
> Job title: {job_title}
> Type of assessment completed: {assessment_type: e.g. written case study / coding test / design task / data exercise}
> Time investment the candidate made (approximate): {time_investment: e.g. 2 to 3 hours / half day}
> Can you share any feedback: {yes — brief / no — legal or process constraint}
> Tone: {appreciative / professional}
>
> Write a 150 to 180 word rejection email. Open by explicitly acknowledging the time they invested in the assessment—name the assessment type. Reject clearly. If feedback is yes, include one to two sentences of honest, constructive feedback without being prescriptive. If feedback is no, acknowledge the absence of feedback is frustrating and explain it briefly. Do not say "unfortunately we cannot share feedback at this time" without explanation.

**When to use it:** Any time a candidate has completed unpaid assessment work. Ghosting after a take-home task is one of the most-cited candidate complaints on Glassdoor. This prompt takes two minutes.

**Pro tip:** If you say "we can't share feedback for legal reasons," that phrase alone frustrates candidates further. The prompt asks ChatGPT to explain the constraint briefly—even something like "our process doesn't allow individual feedback at this stage" lands better than a flat refusal.

## Post-Interview Rejections (First and Second Round)

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email after a first-round interview with the hiring manager.
>
> Candidate first name: {candidate_first_name}
> Job title: {job_title}
> Interviewer name (optional, for personalisation): {interviewer_name}
> Key reason not progressing (internal): {technical_skills_below_bar / cultural_fit_concern / stronger_candidates / communication_style_mismatch}
> Feedback approved to share: {yes — one area / no}
> Tone: {warm / direct / formal}
> Time since interview: {days_since_interview}
>
> Write a 160 to 200 word rejection email. If time since interview is more than 5 days, open with a brief acknowledgement of the delay. Thank them for attending. Reject clearly—do not bury it in paragraph two. If feedback is yes, include one specific, actionable sentence. If feedback is no, do not replace it with vague encouragement. Close warmly. Do not use the phrase "we had many strong candidates."

**When to use it:** After debriefs conclude and the hiring manager has confirmed who progresses. Get these out before the shortlisted candidate accepts—if the role falls through, you may need to re-engage.

**Pro tip:** "We had many strong candidates" is the most hated phrase in recruitment emails according to multiple candidate experience surveys. The constraint in this prompt blocks it. If ChatGPT generates it anyway, add "do not use competitive field language" to your follow-up message.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email after a second-round or panel interview for a professional role.
>
> Candidate first name: {candidate_first_name}
> Job title: {job_title}
> Interview format: {panel_interview / two_stage_sequential / presentation_plus_interview}
> Length of process this candidate completed: {process_length: e.g. 3 interviews over 4 weeks}
> Specific feedback approved to share: {feedback_point_1} and {feedback_point_2}
> Is this candidate worth keeping in the pipeline for future roles: {yes / no}
> Tone: {respectful_and_direct}
>
> Write a 200 to 250 word rejection email. Acknowledge the full length of the process they went through. Reject clearly in the first paragraph. Use the approved feedback points to give two concrete pieces of feedback in a second paragraph—frame them as observations, not judgements. If pipeline is yes, close with a specific invitation to be considered for future roles, not a generic "we'll be in touch." Sign off with HR Manager name placeholder.

**When to use it:** After a lengthy selection process where the candidate invested significant time. A two-sentence rejection after three rounds is one of the fastest ways to generate negative Glassdoor reviews.

**Pro tip:** When providing two feedback points, tell ChatGPT to frame the first as a skills observation and the second as a process or role-fit observation. Mixed feedback types feel more considered and less like a single objection dressed up as balance.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who made it to final round but was not selected—the decision came down to two candidates.
>
> Candidate first name: {candidate_first_name}
> Job title: {job_title}
> What made the decision close: {deciding_factor: e.g. the other candidate had direct sector experience / slightly stronger leadership examples / longer relevant tenure}
> Are you able to share the deciding factor: {yes / no}
> Would you consider this candidate for the next similar opening: {yes / no}
> Tone: {genuinely_warm / professional}
>
> Write a 180 to 220 word rejection email. Be honest that it was a close decision—candidates at final round deserve that context. If the deciding factor can be shared, include it as one clear sentence without comparison language ("the other candidate had X" is not appropriate—reframe as what the role required). If pipeline is yes, make the invitation specific: name the type of role they'd be considered for, not just "future opportunities."

**When to use it:** When you have a strong runner-up and you actually want to keep the relationship. Being specific about why they didn't get it—when you can—converts more candidates into genuine future applicants than vague encouragement.

**Pro tip:** Do not share the deciding factor if it involves any characteristic that could be perceived as discriminatory or if your legal team hasn't cleared it. The {yes/no} variable forces you to make that decision before you generate the email, not after.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate after a competency-based or structured interview where scoring was used.
>
> Candidate first name: {candidate_first_name}
> Job title: {job_title}
> Competency framework used: {competency_framework: e.g. STAR / strength-based / values-based}
> Overall outcome: {did_not_meet_threshold / met_some_not_all / strong_but_outscored}
> One competency area where they were strongest: {strongest_competency}
> One competency area where they fell short of the bar: {gap_competency}
> Tone: {structured / professional}
>
> Write a 170 to 210 word rejection email. Frame the feedback around the competency framework—do not reference numerical scores. Name their strongest competency with one specific observation. Name the gap competency and frame it as an area the role required at a higher level, not a personal failing. Do not say they "didn't demonstrate" anything—use "the role required a higher level of X than came through in our conversation." Close professionally.

**When to use it:** When your hiring process uses scored competency grids and you want feedback that reflects the structured process without exposing raw scores or comparison data.

**Pro tip:** Framing feedback as "the role required X at a higher level" rather than "you didn't demonstrate X" makes the same substantive point with significantly lower risk of the candidate perceiving it as a challenge to dispute.

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to a candidate who interviewed well but whose salary expectations exceeded the approved band.
>
> Candidate first name: {candidate_first_name}
> Job title: {job_title}
> Was salary discussed before interview: {yes / no}
> Can you share the salary band: {yes — share the band / no — do not share figures}
> Is there any flexibility in the band or timeline: {yes — explain briefly / no}
> Tone: {honest / professional / direct}
>
> Write a 140 to 170 word rejection email. Be direct that the reason is compensation misalignment—not a vague "fit" issue. If salary was not discussed before interview, acknowledge that as a process gap without over-apologising. If the band can be shared, include it so they have useful context. If there is no flexibility, say so clearly rather than leaving them uncertain. Do not say "we hope to work together in the future" unless the pipeline is genuinely open.

**When to use it:** When you've had a strong interview process and the candidate is disappointed—this is the scenario most likely to generate a "they wasted my time" Glassdoor review if handled vaguely.

**Pro tip:** If salary was not discussed upfront and that caused the rejection, note that internally. This prompt will generate a polished email, but the real fix is adding salary screening to your process. The email buys goodwill; it doesn't fix the structural gap.

## Internal Candidate Rejections

> You are an HR Manager writing a rejection email to an internal employee who applied for a promotion or lateral move and was not selected.
>
> Employee first name: {employee_first_name}
> Role they applied for: {applied_role}
> Their current role: {current_role}
> Their manager: {line_manager_name}
> Primary reason not selected (internal): {external_hire_stronger / skills_gap / timing / headcount_filled_differently}
> Will you offer a development conversation: {yes / no}
> Tone: {supportive / honest / respectful}
>
> Write a 200 to 240 word email. Acknowledge the courage of applying internally. Reject clearly—do not make them read to the end to find the decision. If a development conversation is offered, make it a concrete invitation with a suggested timeframe, not a vague offer. Do not compare them to the successful candidate. If an external hire was made, you can acknowledge that without framing it as a statement about the employee's value. Note that their line manager {line_manager_name} will also be in touch.

**When to use it:** Before the successful candidate's offer is signed if possible—internal candidates find out through the grapevine within hours. Getting this email out first maintains trust.

**Pro tip:** Never send this email on a Friday afternoon. Internal candidates need time to process during the workday when their manager and HR are available.
